## Harvard Study Reveals US Hyperscale AI Data Center Emissions

### Research in Nature Sustainability links 403 facilities to 25.7 million tons of CO2

A comprehensive study from the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and the Harvard Data Science Initiative has provided the first facility-level accounting of energy use and carbon emissions across 403 U.S. hyperscale data centers supporting artificial intelligence and cloud computing. Published in Nature Sustainability, the peer-reviewed research estimates these facilities consumed between 68 and 99 terawatt-hours of electricity over a one-year period, generating approximately 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide. The findings establish that the physical infrastructure powering modern AI now rivals the electricity consumption of entire American states while drawing more than half of its power from fossil fuels.

## Key details

Researchers led by postdoctoral fellow Gianluca Guidi and senior biostatistics professor Francesca Dominici utilized satellite imagery to identify and verify 403 hyperscale data centers operating across the United States. Combining facility load modeling with regional grid generation data from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) between May 2024 and April 2025, the team assessed both the volume of power consumed and the exact fuel sources generating that electricity.

Under the study's central operating scenario, these 403 facilities accounted for approximately 82 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity, representing roughly 1.8% of total U.S. electrical consumption. Under peak operating assumptions, aggregate demand reached 99 TWh, rivaling the annual power consumption of states such as Indiana, Michigan, or Tennessee. The resulting 25.7 million metric tons of annual CO2 emissions represent a two- to three-fold increase compared to baseline estimates recorded in 2018, matching the total annual carbon footprint of Denmark.

Crucially, the study revealed that roughly 54% of the power supplied to U.S. hyperscale campuses was generated by fossil fuels, with natural gas providing 38% and coal supplying 15%. Approximately one-quarter of all studied data center demand was located within regional grid zones with carbon intensities significantly higher than the national average. Geographic concentration was stark: more than 50% of total hyperscale electricity consumption occurred across just four states—Virginia, Oregon, Ohio, and Iowa.

### Why this matters

The findings challenge industry narratives that hyperscale AI expansion is decoupled from fossil fuel generation or shielded entirely by corporate clean energy credits. Because transmission congestion and grid connection delays frequently prevent new renewable projects from reaching regional hubs, utilities are running legacy coal and natural gas plants harder to meet continuous, round-the-clock data center loads.

Furthermore, by establishing a facility-level baseline, the study provides concrete data for utility regulators and local governments currently grappling with ratepayer impacts. As AI clusters expand from tens of megawatts toward gigawatt-scale campuses, quantifying actual carbon output and fuel reliance helps municipal authorities evaluate whether proposed developments align with local grid reliability and environmental limits.

### Context

Prior to this study, public understanding of data center environmental impacts relied heavily on voluntary, aggregate corporate sustainability disclosures that obscure local grid stresses. The Harvard analysis highlights that the rapid proliferation of generative AI training clusters and large-scale inference hardware has outpaced the deployment of dedicated renewable generation.

The study period concluded in April 2025, meaning its figures reflect an early baseline before the commissioning of newer multi-gigawatt facilities and AI compute clusters planned throughout 2026. The authors noted that the true environmental footprint of hyperscale infrastructure has continued to expand substantially since data collection ended.

## Risks and open questions

The reliance of hyperscale facilities on fossil generation introduces immediate public health questions alongside long-term climate concerns. Coal and natural gas generation emits criteria air pollutants—including fine particulate matter (PM2.5), nitrogen oxides (NOx), and sulfur dioxide (SO2)—that exacerbate cardiovascular and respiratory disease in nearby and downwind communities. The authors noted that direct measurement of these downwind health burdens remains an urgent area for subsequent research.

There are also critical regulatory and transparency questions. Because hyperscale operators generally do not disclose facility-specific hourly load data or water consumption metrics, researchers and grid operators must infer consumption via remote sensing and proxy modeling. Without mandatory reporting standards, state regulators cannot easily verify whether data center clean-energy commitments translate to real emissions reductions on the ground.

## What happens next

The Harvard research team plans to expand its investigation to evaluate direct public health outcomes, air quality impacts, and cooling water consumption across U.S. data center hubs. Meanwhile, state legislators in heavily impacted regions such as Virginia and Oregon are weighing proposed disclosure mandates and infrastructure moratoriums that could require hyperscalers to publicly report facility-level power sources and emissions data before receiving grid interconnection approvals.
