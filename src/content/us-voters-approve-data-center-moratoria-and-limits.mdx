## US Voters Approve Dozens of Local Data Center Bans and Moratoria

### Ballot measures in multiple states restrict facility power, land, and water usage

Voters across dozens of municipalities and counties in the United States approved ballot measures to restrict, delay, or ban data center developments during the November 2026 midterm elections. The widespread approvals reflect rising public resistance against the rapid expansion of AI infrastructure and its heavy demands on local utility grids and municipal water systems.

## Key details

Local election returns tracked across several states revealed broad voter support for limits on large-scale digital infrastructure, with at least 43 separate data center moratorium or restriction measures voted in nationwide. Ohio emerged as a central focal point, passing at least 14 distinct municipal bans or regulations:

- **Strict Municipal Bans:** Ohio communities including Granville, Hubbard, Piqua, Upper Sandusky, and Urbana voted to prohibit new data centers within village boundaries. In California, Alhambra approved a citywide ban, while Edgewater, Florida approved a charter amendment blocking both new facilities and expansions of existing campuses.
- **Power Capacity Caps:** Voters in Conneaut, Defiance, Oregon, and Sunbury, Ohio approved measures prohibiting data centers consuming more than 25MW of power.
- **Water and Acreage Thresholds:** Grove City, Ohio passed a mandate requiring a public vote on any data center project occupying over 50 acres, drawing 20MW or more, or consuming 500,000 gallons of water per day. In Kansas, Emporia voters backed a ban on facilities exceeding 10MW and 100,000 gallons of daily water consumption.
- **Moratoria and Project Blocks:** In Maine, town votes in Peru, Wells, and Eliot established 180-day moratoria, while Sabattus enacted a moratorium on facilities exceeding 10,000 square feet or 20MW until May 2027. Voters in Ashville, Ohio rejected a planned term sheet for two EdgeConneX facilities, and Bloomington, Illinois backed an advisory moratorium on large-scale campuses.

Exit polling conducted across 45 states underscored the political weight of infrastructure issues, with 56 percent of surveyed voters identifying AI data centers as a crucial or major factor influencing their Congressional vote.

### Why this matters

The success of these ballot measures signals a direct shift from administrative zoning reviews to direct voter action against AI resource consumption. As hyperscalers and cloud operators rush to secure gigawatt-scale capacity for AI training and inference, local populations are pushing back against the localized externalities of that compute—specifically high electricity consumption, substation congestion, and substantial cooling water withdrawals.

These restrictions constrain site availability across established secondary and tertiary data center markets, forcing developers to contend with strict capacity ceilings such as 20MW or 25MW limits. By capping facility scale and mandating public referendums for projects exceeding daily water usage thresholds, local communities are establishing legal barriers that increase site selection friction and raise infrastructure delivery timelines.

### Context

Over the preceding two years, rapid construction of AI-focused campuses created friction with regional power grids such as PJM and ERCOT. In several regions, utilities sought substantial rate adjustments or capital spending programs to accommodate rising industrial loads, triggering ratepayer backlash. Municipalities also faced concerns regarding cooling water consumption during drought conditions, leading grassroots coalitions to organize ballot initiatives to reclaim local zoning authority.

Earlier in 2026, state-level discussions and legislative proposals in states like California, Pennsylvania, and Virginia explored ratepayer protections and grid connection surcharges. However, the November 2026 ballot results demonstrate that municipal and township voters are unwilling to wait for federal or state oversight, opting instead to deploy local zoning laws and binding charter amendments to curb hyperscale expansion.

## Risks and open questions

The primary uncertainty following these votes centers on legal durability and potential preemption challenges. Several restrictions, including municipal bans passed in Kansas jurisdictions such as Emporia and Ottawa, face ongoing judicial reviews to determine whether municipal ballot initiatives can override preexisting state utility agreements or regional economic development statutes.

Additionally, developers facing local moratoria may pivot toward unincorporated county lands or negotiate behind-the-meter generation deals with private power producers, potentially shifting environmental and grid burdens rather than eliminating them.

## What happens next

Developers with projects currently in planning pipelines will need to review approved municipal limits to evaluate compliance with new power and water caps. Operators whose projects exceed the newly established 20MW or 25MW ceilings will either need to downscale facility designs or seek alternate locations in neighboring jurisdictions.

In towns where moratorium windows were enacted, local planning boards and councils will use the temporary pauses to draft formal zoning ordinances and resource-reporting standards for data centers before moratoria expire in 2027.
