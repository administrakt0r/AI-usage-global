## North Carolina Rejects Clean Tariff Mandate Amid 9.5% Duke Rate Hike

### State regulators decline to force monopoly utility to offer clean power contracts to tech giants while approving residential rate increases.

North Carolina utility regulators declined on November 16 to order Duke Energy to create a binding clean energy purchasing tariff for tech companies, according to reporting by [Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/clean-energy/north-carolina-clean-transition-tariff-punt). The decision leaves data center developers without a dedicated route to buy clean power in the state. At the same time, the commission approved a 9.5% average rate hike on residential households. Tech operators expanding in the Carolinas must now rely on Duke's standard generation mix or wait for voluntary utility programs.

## Key details

The [North Carolina Utilities Commission](https://www.ncuc.gov/) issued its multi-year rate order covering Duke Energy Carolinas and Duke Energy Progress. Regulators rejected petitions from clean energy advocates and corporate buyers who sought a mandatory deadline for a Clean Transition Tariff. Instead, the commission stated that it encouraged Duke to propose a tariff, declining to set a binding schedule.

The ruling comes despite formal requests from the Corporate Energy Buyers Association, which represents major technology firms including Google, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta. Tech hyperscalers sought the tariff because state law forces commercial users to buy electricity directly from regulated monopoly utilities. Under a clean transition tariff, corporate buyers pay the full cost of new solar, storage, and clean firm generation so other customers do not subsidize their load.

Duke previously offered an alternative green tariff called Green Source Advantage Choice in 2023. That program included a mechanism for corporate buyers to fund accelerated clean energy construction. Over two years, zero corporate customers applied due to restrictive terms and prohibitive fees.

Under the new order, the commission approved Duke's request to raise residential rates by 9.5% over two years. Regulators also established a distinct large-load service class for data centers with demand exceeding 100 megawatts. New facilities must sign 15-year service contracts and pay upfront for substation work and local transmission additions.

### By the numbers

- **9.5%**: Average electricity rate increase approved for Duke Energy residential customers over the next two years.
- **100 MW**: Minimum demand threshold triggering specialized large-load data center interconnection rules.
- **15 years**: Minimum contract duration mandated for new hyperscale facilities connecting to the Duke Energy grid.
- **0**: Corporate customers that applied for Duke's resource acceleration tariff option since 2023.

### Why this matters

The decision sharpens tension between tech companies seeking round-the-clock clean power and Southeast utilities that rely on fossil generation. Deprived of a mechanism to add clean capacity, new data center facilities draw directly from Duke's existing generation fleet.

The Charlotte-based utility has repeatedly pointed to projected data center expansion to justify plans for new gas turbines. In September, state regulators rejected Duke's bid to fast-track three gas peaker units, ruling that the utility had not proven data center demand justified billions in capital outlays. That ruling, covered in [earlier reporting on Duke's gas turbine denial](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/north-carolina-rejects-duke-energy-gas-turbine-over-staggering-costs), showed the danger of building fossil assets for tech loads that could leave households paying off stranded plants.

Barred from funding dedicated clean generation, hyperscalers end up drawing from the general grid. Their demand accelerates total electricity consumption and drives system-wide transmission upgrades that flow into general rate cases.

### Context

Clean transition tariffs are spreading across regulated electricity markets. In 2024, Google worked with NV Energy in Nevada to create a tariff allowing commercial customers to procure clean firm power. Indiana and Minnesota regulators are considering similar frameworks, while Georgia Power established a customer-identified resource program to accommodate large data centers.

North Carolina remains a primary data center target in the Southeast. Demand projections from Duke suggest regional commercial load will grow by several gigawatts by 2030, driven by artificial intelligence workloads and advanced manufacturing.

## Risks and open questions

The main risk is that tech operators will accept standard grid power rather than delay project schedules, increasing emissions from coal and gas units. Alternatively, companies may bypass North Carolina and build campuses in states with competitive power markets or established green tariffs.

The order also provides no timeline for voluntary action. Duke told regulators it plans to hold talks with customers in early 2027, but the ruling imposes no penalties if those talks stall.

## What happens next

Duke is expected to start informal stakeholder discussions with corporate buyers in the first quarter of 2027. Separately, a bipartisan energy task force formed by Governor Josh Stein will deliver policy recommendations to the North Carolina General Assembly in February 2027. Those recommendations could include legislative directives governing data center clean power procurement and transmission cost allocation.
