## US Bill Introduced to Ban Data Centers on Federal Lands

### Proposed H.R. 11002 targets water, grid, and emissions strains from tech expansion

A new legislative measure introduced in the U.S. House of Representatives would prohibit the construction of commercial data centers on millions of acres of federally managed public lands. Titled the Protecting Our Public Lands from Data Centers Act (H.R. 11002), the bill directly targets the surging land, energy, and cooling water consumption required by artificial intelligence and cloud infrastructure. If enacted, the legislation would preempt federal initiatives aimed at opening public conservation areas and agency reservations to private computing facilities.

## Key details

Introduced by Representative Maxine Dexter (D-OR) and co-sponsored by 13 House lawmakers, H.R. 11002 would bar the development, sale, or transfer of covered federal lands nationwide for new commercial data centers and associated energy infrastructure. The measure applies specifically to federal acreage managed by the U.S. Forest Service, within the Department of Agriculture, and the Department of the Interior, including the Bureau of Land Management (BLM). To prevent regulatory evasion, the bill establishes a strict statutory reversion clause: any covered parcel sold or transferred after enactment that is subsequently converted into a data center without explicit congressional approval would automatically revert to federal ownership.

The legislative push responds directly to recent federal initiatives seeking to relieve power bottlenecks by utilizing government property. The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) has previously identified multiple sites capable of supporting gigawatt-scale computing clusters, facilitating multi-hundred-megawatt proposals such as Brookfield’s planned developments in Kentucky and SoftBank’s initiatives on DOE land in Ohio. Similarly, the Department of Defense has explored making military properties available to private infrastructure developers, including projects evaluated by Carlyle and CyrusOne in Texas and Utah.

Lawmakers and a broad coalition of environmental organizations—including the Sierra Club, the Center for Biological Diversity, Earthjustice Action, and WaterWatch of Oregon—voiced alarm over the cumulative resource strain of hyperscale computing facilities. They cited severe pressures on municipal water utilities and protected regional watersheds that supply drinking water, the risk of grid instability from multi-hundred-megawatt continuous loads, and localized air quality degradation from on-site natural gas generation deployed to bypass utility interconnection delays.

### Why this matters

As artificial intelligence model training and inference outpace available electrical substation capacity, tech operators are searching for unconventional development pathways. Siting campuses on public lands offers developers vast contiguous parcels and potential fast-track permitting, but it shifts the environmental and resource costs of high-density computing onto public ecosystems and nearby communities.

Large-scale AI data centers require substantial resource inputs, frequently demanding tens of millions of gallons of cooling water annually alongside continuous power supplies ranging from 100 megawatts to upwards of a gigawatt per campus. By closing federal public lands to commercial data center leasing, H.R. 11002 would force technology firms to negotiate within private property boundaries and municipal utility constraints, ensuring that federal conservation acreage and critical public watersheds are not converted into industrial power sinks.

### Context

The federal proposal reflects escalating public and state-level pushback against data center resource demands across the Pacific Northwest and the broader United States. In Oregon, Governor Tina Kotek enacted an executive directive freezing the sale, lease, and easement of state-owned properties for data center developments through July 1, 2027, while directing state regulators to temporarily halt air quality pollution permits for new facilities. Municipalities and county voters across Ohio, Texas, and Virginia have similarly enacted local zoning restrictions and power caps on high-density data centers.

Simultaneously, utility grid operators face multi-year queues to interconnect new generation and transmission infrastructure. The bottleneck has prompted tech operators to increasingly propose behind-the-meter fossil generation—such as dedicated natural gas turbines—to operationalize AI hardware quickly. Environmental advocates emphasize that permitting such fossil-fueled complexes on public lands would directly contradict federal decarbonization mandates.

## Risks and open questions

The primary open question is whether H.R. 11002 can secure sufficient bipartisan backing to advance through Congress amid competing pressures to accelerate domestic AI leadership. Opponents of federal leasing restrictions argue that utilizing brownfield government reservations, such as decommissioned nuclear and defense sites, relieves land pressure on suburban communities and leverages preexisting electrical substations.

Additionally, the bill exempts lands held in trust for Indigenous Tribes, pueblos, and rancherias, leaving questions regarding how tribal sovereignty and regional energy development will intersect. If federal public lands are permanently placed off-limits, developers may intensify land acquisitions in rural agricultural regions, shifting grid strain, water table depletion, and local tax battles to municipal governments with limited regulatory oversight.

## What happens next

The bill has been referred to relevant House committees for initial consideration and legislative hearings. Environmental and conservation coalitions plan to lobby for a companion measure in the U.S. Senate, seeking to expand protections across federal jurisdictions.

In the near term, federal agencies—including the Department of Energy and the Department of Defense—continue to assess private developer proposals for existing pilot reservations. Observers and energy analysts will closely monitor whether the legislative scrutiny prompts federal agencies to impose stricter environmental reviews, mandatory water usage disclosures, and clean energy standards on any pending infrastructure leases.
