## Indiana Approves $74M Grid Surcharge for Google Data Center

### Regulators allow AES Indiana to allocate transmission upgrade costs across all customers

The Indiana Utility Regulatory Commission approved a contested service agreement between AES Indiana and Google to power a planned 390 MW data center in Monrovia, Indiana. The ruling allows the utility to add $73.8 million in transmission network upgrades to its general rate base, shifting significant grid infrastructure costs driven by computational demand onto ordinary ratepayers.

## Key details

Under the approved Monrovia Project plan, AES Indiana will build or procure new generation, battery storage, and transmission infrastructure to support Google's 390 MW data center campus at an estimated total cost of $737 million. The utility will construct three dedicated clean energy and storage resources: the 200 MW Foundry Works battery energy storage system, the 130 MW Hardy Hills battery facility, and the Crosstrack project, which pairs a 130 MW solar installation with a 130 MW four-hour battery storage system.

The transmission infrastructure required for the facility is split into direct connection equipment and wider regional grid improvements. While Google has agreed to pay approximately $32 million upfront to cover the direct interconnection to the facility, state regulators ruled that $73.8 million in regional network upgrades will be placed into AES Indiana's regulated transmission rate base. Those network costs, including a major new substation engineered to handle up to 1,200 MW of future load, will be recovered from all utility customers, including residential households.

The regulatory decision passed by a unanimous 5-0 vote. Commissioners concluded that even if Google's computing facility was the sole impetus for constructing the grid enhancements, the new transmission capacity provides system-wide reliability benefits that justify socialized cost recovery across the utility's entire service territory. Consumer advocacy groups, including the Citizens Action Coalition, vigorously contested the arrangement, arguing that spreading these network costs to the public violates state standards and contradicts tech industry promises to protect consumers from rising power bills.

### Why this matters

The decision highlights a contentious loophole in data center cost allocation and ratepayer protection policies. Although Indiana state law requires large-load commercial customers to reimburse at least 80% of project costs reasonably allocable to them, categorizing heavy grid reinforcements as broad "reliability upgrades" permits utilities to socialize dozens of millions of dollars in capital expenses. As tech hyperscalers contract multi-hundred-megawatt computing facilities, the burden of funding high-voltage transmission lines and multi-gigawatt substations is increasingly being transferred to local residential ratepayers.

### Context

Across the United States, mounting public backlash over rising electric utility bills has prompted intense scrutiny of hyperscale AI data centers. Earlier in 2026, technology companies and major utilities signed voluntary commitments such as the Ratepayer Protection Pledge, promising that large computational loads would bear their own infrastructure costs. However, regulatory frameworks vary widely by state, and state commissions frequently struggle to disentangle dedicated data center connections from general transmission expansion approved by regional transmission organizations like the Midcontinent Independent System Operator.

## Risks and open questions

The primary risk is that utility rate cases will continue to quietly shift regional expansion costs onto residential consumers through redacted service contracts. Key commercial provisions of the AES Indiana agreement with Google, including specific power rates, monthly demand charges, and rider details, remain confidential. Consumer advocates warn that without legislative transparency mandates, ratepayers will be forced to shoulder supplemental costs associated with transmission congestion, wholesale price spikes, and secondary grid reinforcements without realizing net economic benefits.

## What happens next

AES Indiana will move forward with engineering and construction of the Monrovia Project's solar and battery assets, while MISO coordinates the broader transmission interconnection schedule. The utility plans to submit a comprehensive cost-of-service study, cost allocation report, and updated rate design principles during its next general rate case scheduled for 2029. Meanwhile, consumer protection groups in Indiana are preparing legislative proposals to tighten the definition of allocable large-load costs before the state's upcoming legislative session.
