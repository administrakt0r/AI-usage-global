## US Senate Passes BAAJA Bill Requiring 20MW Data Centers to Fund Grid

### Bipartisan legislation directs FERC to mandate cost allocation for large computing facilities and accelerates transmission permitting

The US Senate approved the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 in a 62–33 vote on November 20, 2026. The legislation directs the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission to create binding cost-allocation rules for data centers requiring more than 20 megawatts of capacity. Under the mandate, hyperscale operators must pay for the high-voltage transmission lines and substation upgrades required to connect their facilities rather than shifting those expenses onto utility ratepayers.

## Key details

The bill, known as BAAJA, was co-authored by Republican Senators Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia and Mike Lee of Utah, alongside Democratic Senators Martin Heinrich of New Mexico and Sheldon Whitehouse of Rhode Island. [Data Center Dynamics reported](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/us-senate-passes-bill-requiring-data-centers-to-pay-more-for-grid-infrastructure/) that the bill grants FERC expanded authority to issue federal construction permits for interstate electric transmission lines, overriding local and state-level siting bottlenecks.

Beyond electrical cost allocation, the legislation establishes strict statutory timelines for federal reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act. It shortens environmental assessment windows, curbs court challenges, and limits the ability of agencies to revoke permits once work begins. It also exempts certain energy projects, pipeline expansions within existing rights-of-way, and non-federal onshore drilling from standard review hurdles, creating an expedited pipeline for both clean energy and fossil generation.

### Why this matters

Data center developers in major hubs have requested tens of gigawatts of grid interconnections, causing wholesale power prices to spike and forcing regional grid operators to plan billions of dollars in network upgrades. When utilities finance new transmission lines through traditional rate bases, residential households and small businesses absorb higher monthly power bills. By requiring facilities drawing 20 MW or more to directly fund network enhancements, the legislation aims to protect consumers from the capital expenses of tech expansion.

At the same time, grid operators like PJM Interconnection have warned that transmission bottlenecks threaten system reliability as artificial intelligence clusters come online. Accelerating interstate transmission permitting provides a legal mechanism to build the regional lines required to wheel electricity to computing hubs.

### Context

Congressional action follows months of contentious debate over digital infrastructure. In October 2026, [Senate Democrats blocked the Ratepayer Protection Act](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/us-senate-blocks-ratepayer-protection-act) in a 57–43 vote because that proposal relied on voluntary commitments without statutory enforcement. BAAJA replaces voluntary pledges with an explicit directive for FERC to establish regulatory standards.

The bill has produced an unusual split among energy and climate groups. Clean energy advocacy groups, including Evergreen Action, supported the bill because the transmission overhaul enables new wind and solar generation to connect to the grid. In contrast, environmental groups such as Earthjustice and the Sierra Club opposed the measure, warning that shortening review windows and easing fossil fuel permitting will increase industrial emissions and lock in natural gas generation for computing campuses.

## Risks and open questions

The legislation faces an uncertain path in the House of Representatives, where Republicans hold a narrow majority and several conservative lawmakers oppose federal transmission preemption over state utility regulators. Conversely, progressive House members have threatened to oppose the bill unless the environmental permitting waivers and fossil fuel exemptions are stripped out.

Additionally, the text directs FERC to create ratepayer protections but leaves the specific tariff structures, cost formulas, and capacity thresholds to the commission's rulemaking process. If FERC allows broad exceptions or sets lenient reimbursement formulas, utilities could still shift indirect capacity costs to the public.

## What happens next

The BAAJA legislation moves to the House floor for consideration. If the House passes the bill without amendments, it will head to the president's desk for signature. If the House amends the text, lawmakers must reconcile differences in conference committee before the current legislative session adjourns.
