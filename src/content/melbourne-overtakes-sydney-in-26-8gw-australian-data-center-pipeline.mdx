## Melbourne Overtakes Sydney in 26.8GW Australian Data Center Pipeline

### Grid Bottlenecks Shift Australian AI Compute to Victoria as Pipeline Hits 26.8 GW

Australia's tracked data center development pipeline expanded by 5.2 GW over six months to reach 26.8 GW, propelled by surging demand for artificial intelligence workloads and cloud capacity, according to the latest census by DC Byte and Data Centres Australia. In a historic shift, Melbourne has overtaken Sydney as the nation's largest development market, adding 1.7 GW to reach 10.7 GW while Sydney contracted by 3.6 GW to 7.8 GW under mounting power constraints. Yet operational capacity rose by just 0.1 GW to 1.6 GW nationwide, underscoring an acute execution bottleneck where 94% of planned capacity remains in pre-operational planning.

## Key details

The bi-annual census tracks 163 operational facilities alongside 97 proposed projects. Megacampuses dominate the pipeline: 52 of the 97 proposed developments feature announced capacity exceeding 100 MW, compared to only four operational data centers of that scale today.

Early-stage proposals account for 19.6 GW, or 73% of Australia's 26.8 GW total. In contrast, only 0.4 GW is currently under active construction—defined by [DC Byte](https://www.dcbyte.com) as data halls undergoing mechanical and electrical fit-out. Teresa Tan, DC Byte's lead analyst for Asia-Pacific, noted that announcements outpace buildouts: "A developer can add an entire campus to the pipeline in one update, but from an executional perspective, capacity comes online one data hall at a time."

The divergence between Australia's two primary hubs reflects shifting state planning rules and transmission limits, as reported by [Data Center Knowledge](https://www.datacenterknowledge.com/data-center-site-selection/melbourne-overtakes-sydney-in-australia-s-data-center-pipeline). Melbourne moved 1.2 GW into committed capacity, aided by Victoria's Development Facilitation Program. Meanwhile, Sydney's early-stage pipeline dropped from 8.6 GW to 4.2 GW as developers withdrew speculative applications, though committed capacity rose to 2.6 GW and Sydney maintains more operating capacity (0.9 GW) than Melbourne (0.5 GW).

### By the numbers

- **26.8 GW**: Total tracked data center capacity in Australia, up 5.2 GW in six months.
- **10.7 GW**: Tracked pipeline in Melbourne, surpassing Sydney (7.8 GW) for the first time.
- **1.6 GW**: Operational capacity nationwide, up just 0.1 GW since April.
- **0.4 GW**: Capacity currently under active mechanical and electrical construction.
- **52**: Proposed campuses exceeding 100 MW, versus 4 operating facilities today.
- **3.9 GW**: Projected operational capacity across Australia by 2030.
- **31%**: Share of the pipeline located outside Sydney and Melbourne, up from 19% two years ago.

### Why this matters

The gap between Australia's 26.8 GW pipeline and 1.6 GW of operating capacity highlights the key constraint facing AI expansion: site-level grid connections. While the Australian electricity market has sufficient bulk generation, delivering power to high-density compute clusters requires new terminal stations and substation upgrades that face multi-year lead times.

Dr. William Lee, senior research director at IDC, emphasized that connection queues are the defining hurdle: "Australia's challenge before 2030 is connecting individual sites, not generating enough electricity." Without secured terminal station slots, announced campuses risk prolonged delays.

### Context

Sydney's contraction follows tightening grid limits and municipal resistance. Developers have faced mounting frictions, prompting withdrawals such as [Goodman Group abandoning its proposed 90MW Sydney AI campus](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/goodman-withdraws-application-for-90mw-sydney-ai-data-center). To manage grid strain, New South Wales introduced rules requiring data centers seeking expedited 75-day assessments to secure net-positive energy and water contributions.

Developers are expanding into regional markets, which now represent 31% of the pipeline. While regions like the Latrobe Valley offer land, high-voltage infrastructure and water availability remain unproven at scale.

## Risks and open questions

The primary uncertainty is how much of the 19.6 GW early-stage pipeline represents genuine delivery intent rather than speculative queue-squatting. Furthermore, global lead times for high-voltage transformers and switchgear now stretch to three or four years.

While operators are deploying closed-loop and air cooling to satisfy water restrictions, intense summer heatwaves in inland areas present efficiency and cooling challenges.

## What happens next

DC Byte forecasts Australia's operational capacity will reach 3.9 GW by 2030, with Sydney reaching 2.0 GW and Melbourne reaching 1.4 GW. 

Grid operators and state authorities are tightening connection rules throughout 2027, requiring binding customer commitments and equipment delivery slots before granting substation access.
