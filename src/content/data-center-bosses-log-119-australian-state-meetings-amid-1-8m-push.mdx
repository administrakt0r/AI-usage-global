## Data Center Bosses Log 119 Australian State Meetings Amid $1.8M Push

### Ministerial diaries reveal intensive lobbying across five states as power and planning disputes delay projects

Executives from 10 data center operators logged 119 closed-door meetings with Australian state premiers and ministers over the past three years as industry political donations nearly tripled to $1.8 million. An analysis of public ministerial diaries by Transparency International Australia reveals that operators intensified high-level lobbying in 2026 to secure fast-tracked planning and grid connections. The political push coincides with mounting community resistance over transmission infrastructure, power capacity, and water usage in key expansion hubs.

## Key details

The analysis tracked disclosed ministerial diaries across the five Australian jurisdictions that publish them: New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania, Queensland, and the Australian Capital Territory. Other states, including Western Australia and South Australia, do not release ministerial diaries, so the recorded numbers understate total government contacts.

According to [Transparency International Australia](https://www.transparency.org.au/), donations from major data center operators and contracted lobbying firms jumped from $685,000 to $1.8 million over the three-year period. Across the broader digital infrastructure sector, political contributions totaled $3.4 million over four years.

The three companies securing the most ministerial meetings were Amazon Web Services, Macquarie Technology Group, and NextDC, Australia's largest domestic operator. Queensland Premier David Crisafulli was the most frequently lobbied leader, with 13 meetings over three years.

In Queensland, NextDC met with Crisafulli three times in August and September 2026. Shortly afterward, the state government proposed taking data center planning approvals away from local municipal councils to fast-track approvals. Crisafulli also traveled to the United States in September to court a proposed $31.9 billion project with Anthropic.

In Tasmania, Firmus Technologies logged 10 meetings with ministers and the premier in 2026 while seeking planning approvals amid local resistance over power and water use.

### Why this matters

The lobbying wave tracks mounting friction between data center power demands and local electrical grids. In Melbourne, NextDC had an expansion halted after network providers paused work on high-voltage power poles due to community protests. Similar grid and water disputes have surfaced in regional New South Wales and Tasmania.

To avoid municipal planning delays, developers seek state-level intervention. Stripping local councils of planning powers or classifying facilities as state-significant infrastructure allows operators to override local environmental concerns and lock in scarce transmission capacity.

### Context

Australia faces a sharp surge in proposed data center capacity. Developers have proposed [26.8GW of data center capacity across Australia](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/melbourne-overtakes-sydney-in-26-8gw-australian-data-center-pipeline), but only 1.6GW is currently operational.

With urban substations in Sydney and Melbourne facing multi-year interconnection queues, operators increasingly rely on direct political lobbying to secure power. Federal lobbying remains unmonitored because federal ministers do not release diaries, and disclosure laws exclude in-house corporate lobbyists.

## Risks and open questions

The main risk is the loss of local community oversight over power and land. Preempting municipal councils removes public hearings on noise, water use, and grid impacts.

Major disclosure gaps remain. The 119 meetings exclude non-publishing states and all federal interactions. At NextDC's annual general meeting, Chair Stephen Flynn noted that the sector historically operated out of public sight, but conceded digital infrastructure can no longer remain invisible as power demands grow.

## What happens next

Queensland and New South Wales continue developing frameworks to speed up data center permits. Transparency International Australia is using the findings to press for federal disclosure laws covering in-house lobbyists. Meanwhile, community opposition to transmission lines in Victoria and Tasmania will test whether state backing can bypass local resistance.
