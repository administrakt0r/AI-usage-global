## Google Signs 50MW Superhot Rock Geothermal Deal in Oregon

### Deal with Quaise Energy will supply 24/7 carbon-free energy from Newberry Volcano to power The Dalles data centers by 2030

Google has signed a power purchase agreement to buy 50 megawatts of continuous electricity from Quaise Energy's Project Obsidian, a pioneering superhot rock geothermal project located on the southern flank of the Newberry Volcano in Central Oregon. The agreement represents the world's first commercial power contract for superhot rock geothermal energy and will supply round-the-clock clean electricity to power three of Google's data center campuses in The Dalles starting as early as 2030. The move comes as hyperscale cloud operators race to procure firm, zero-carbon power to satisfy surging artificial intelligence workloads without running afoul of regional clean energy mandates or intensifying grid strain.

## Key details

Project Obsidian is situated on federal geothermal leases in the Deschutes National Forest, approximately 30 miles south of Bend, Oregon. Superhot rock geothermal operates by drilling several miles deep into the Earth's crust to reach volcanic formations with temperatures approaching 800 degrees Fahrenheit (approximately 425 degrees Celsius). Once reached, water is pumped deep underground through fractured crystalline rock, heated to supercritical states by subterranean thermal energy, and brought back to the surface as high-temperature steam to drive electrical turbines.

Cambridge-based Quaise Energy, spun out of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 2018, is utilizing a hybrid drilling approach to reach these extreme depths. The project's initial development phase deploys conventional rotary drilling, while subsequent deep-phase drilling will use proprietary millimeter-wave gyrotron technology designed to vaporize hard volcanic basalt and granite without mechanical contact. By eliminating mechanical drill-bit wear and downhole replacement delays, Quaise aims to access depths between 3 and 12 miles where superhot conditions exist globally.

Under the agreement, Google will purchase 50 megawatts of baseload capacity—an electrical volume equivalent to the continuous power demand of approximately 50,000 average American homes. To deliver the power to the regional grid, Quaise must construct a 10-mile transmission line from the Newberry Volcano site to the nearest electrical substation in La Pine, Oregon. From La Pine, the power will route northward across the regional transmission network to Google's data centers in Wasco County along the Columbia River. Quaise estimates that the Newberry volcanic basin contains up to 10 gigawatts of exploitable thermal potential, with Project Obsidian targeting roughly 1 gigawatt over the next decade.

### Why this matters

Surging artificial intelligence training and inference workloads require continuous, 24/7 electrical power with near-perfect availability. While hyperscalers have historically matched annual electricity consumption using intermittent solar and wind credits, regional grids cannot support high-density AI data halls with intermittent generation alone without costly battery banks or natural-gas backup plants.

In Oregon, state climate legislation mandates that retail electricity suppliers eliminate 100 percent of greenhouse gas emissions by 2040, limiting tech companies from deploying on-site fossil generation. By serving as the commercial anchor customer for superhot rock geothermal, Google is injecting private capital into a nascent technology that could produce up to 10 times more clean electricity per well than traditional geothermal systems, potentially transforming how hyperscalers secure round-the-clock power in constrained transmission corridors.

### Context

Google's global electricity consumption surged by 37 percent in 2025, driven primarily by data center expansions to support generative AI infrastructure. The search giant has previously contracted 396 megawatts of enhanced geothermal power from Fervo Energy's Cape Station in southwestern Utah and tested a smaller 3.5 MW geothermal pilot in Nevada.

However, superhot rock geothermal represents an order-of-magnitude leap in thermodynamic efficiency compared to conventional enhanced geothermal systems. While existing EGS facilities circulate water through rock formations between 300 and 400 degrees Fahrenheit, superhot rock exceeds 705 degrees Fahrenheit, the critical point where water enters a supercritical state. Supercritical fluid behaves like a dense gas with the heat-carrying capacity of a liquid, allowing each well to extract significantly more energy with smaller surface footprints. The deal comes amid regional power concerns in the Pacific Northwest, where federal grid planners forecast an 11-gigawatt regional power deficit by 2032 due to rapid industrial and data center load growth.

## Risks and open questions

Transmission capacity and grid interconnection remain the most critical hurdles for Project Obsidian. The Bonneville Power Administration (BPA), which controls approximately 75 percent of the high-voltage transmission grid in the Pacific Northwest, constructed only one mile of new transmission line between 2020 and 2025 due to permitting bottlenecks and funding constraints. Interconnecting the 10-mile spur into the La Pine substation will require extensive federal environmental reviews through the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA).

Technological scalability also remains unproven at commercial utility scale. While Quaise has demonstrated millimeter-wave rock vaporization in laboratory and shallow field tests, maintaining beam coherence and borehole stability at depths exceeding five miles in high-pressure volcanic environments presents severe engineering challenges. Additionally, operating high-pressure closed-loop water injection in seismically active volcanic regions requires rigorous microseismic monitoring to prevent induced seismicity and protect local groundwater resources.

## What happens next

Quaise Energy will proceed with initial conventional drilling and geophysical logging at the Newberry Volcano lease area while finalizing engineering specifications for its millimeter-wave deep-drilling equipment. The company will also advance permit applications with the U.S. Forest Service and the Bureau of Land Management for the 10-mile transmission corridor linking Project Obsidian to the La Pine substation.

Google will continue evaluating advanced firm clean energy contracts across North America to support its expanding fleet of hyperscale AI compute clusters, while regional utilities and competing data center operators monitor Project Obsidian as a benchmark for next-generation baseload geothermal power.
