## Chemours Launches Faizeon Two-Phase Liquid Cooling for AI Hardware

### Dielectric fluids aim to curb rising data center water use and thermal strain

Chemical manufacturer Chemours has launched a new range of dielectric fluids designed for two-phase direct-to-chip and immersion cooling systems in artificial intelligence data centers. The product family, branded as Faizeon, targets thermal management constraints as high-density AI accelerators push server heat loads past the limits of conventional air and single-phase liquid cooling.

## Key details

Chemours announced the introduction of its Faizeon dielectric fluids to support high-power computing processors and servers. According to the company, testing demonstrated that its Faizeon 200 fluid maintained a graphics processing unit (GPU) at operating temperatures up to 19°C (66°F) lower than a single-phase water-based fluid while requiring a lower coolant flow rate.

While the majority of high-density AI data centers currently relying on liquid cooling deploy single-phase direct-to-chip systems, industry architects anticipate that two-phase cooling—where liquid absorbs processor heat and vaporizes before condensing in a closed loop—will become necessary for next-generation AI accelerators. Chemours chief executive Denise Dignam noted that rapid AI expansion is turning cooling into a critical infrastructure constraint, stating that the fluids aim to manage elevated heat loads while cutting operational energy and water consumption.

### Why this matters

Data center cooling accounts for significant portions of overall facility power and utility water consumption. Conventional evaporative cooling towers can consume millions of gallons of water daily, drawing intense pushback from municipalities facing drought conditions and constrained water supplies. Two-phase dielectric cooling offers higher heat transfer efficiency at lower volumetric flow rates, allowing hyperscale operators to cool kilowatt-scale server racks without relying on massive evaporative water consumption or oversized mechanical chiller infrastructure.

### Context

As frontier AI training clusters scale toward gigawatt capacity and individual chip power envelopes exceed 1,000 watts, thermal dissipation has become an engineering bottleneck alongside electricity availability. Single-phase water loops face physical limits in extracting localized heat flux from dense semiconductor packages without substantial pumping power and extensive piping. A transition to two-phase dielectric fluids mirrors the broader data center shift toward closed-loop thermal systems that decouple compute density from municipal water grids.

## Risks and open questions

The transition to two-phase liquid cooling introduces operational and regulatory hurdles. Dielectric fluids must demonstrate long-term chemical stability, material compatibility with server components, and non-toxicity across prolonged operational cycles. In addition, data center operators face significant retrofit costs and supply-chain retooling to transition existing facility infrastructure from single-phase cold plates to sealed, pressure-managed two-phase fluid systems.

## What happens next

Data center operators and server original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) are expected to evaluate two-phase fluid performance across upcoming accelerator generations. As computing facilities face strict local water permits and energy efficiency mandates, the adoption speed of two-phase direct-to-chip and immersion solutions will test whether chemical innovations can alleviate the physical resource constraints facing large-scale AI infrastructure.
