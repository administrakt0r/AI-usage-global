## IEA Projects 70% Surge in Data Center Carbon Emissions by 2030

### Expanding US gas pipelines and Chinese coal generation to power AI infrastructure threaten global climate targets

Carbon emissions from global data centres will jump 70% by 2030 as operators rely heavily on fossil fuels to power artificial intelligence, according to the International Energy Agency. In its annual energy assessment, the Paris-based agency warned that electricity use by data centres will double over the next four years, with AI model training and inference accounting for three-quarters of that growth. The findings highlight a widening gap between corporate clean energy pledges and the physical generation capacity actually connecting to power grids.

## Key details

The [International Energy Agency](https://www.iea.org/reports/world-energy-outlook-2026) projects that dedicated AI workloads will add 350 terawatt-hours of annual power demand by 2030. That increase alone exceeds the entire annual electricity consumption of Great Britain. While tech companies remain the largest corporate purchasers of renewable power contracts, hardware deployment is outpacing clean generation queues, pushing developers toward fossil fuel interconnections.

Grid reliance varies sharply across major regions. American data centres will lean heavily on natural gas, with the fuel projected to supply roughly 60% of their electricity by 2030. Agency analysts tracked plans for approximately 180 gigawatts of new gas-fired capacity linked directly to US data centres as of August 2026, an amount comparable to the entire installed natural gas fleet of the European Union. In China, coal-fired power plants are slated to supply roughly half the electricity consumed by AI facilities by 2030, with renewables, nuclear, and minimal gas making up the balance. Conversely, the European Union is projected to power 70% of its data centres using renewable generation by 2030, followed by nuclear and gas.

To build these projections, the IEA analyzed satellite imagery across 93 global "AI factories" and tracked hardware supply chains. Global shipments of dedicated AI servers have climbed eighteen-fold since 2020. Operational capacity dedicated strictly to AI facilities expanded tenfold over the past 24 months to 17 gigawatts. Once the 93 campuses currently under construction finish development, their collective capacity will reach roughly 50 gigawatts.

### By the numbers

- **70%**: projected increase in global data centre carbon emissions by 2030.
- **350 TWh**: additional annual electricity consumed by AI facilities worldwide within four years.
- **180 GW**: proposed gas-fired generation capacity linked to US data centres.
- **120 GW**: existing global data centre capacity, with an additional 120 GW projected under baseline forecasts.
- **18-fold**: growth in annual global AI server shipments since 2020.

### Why this matters

Deploying fossil generation at this scale undermines utility decarbonization timelines and degrades regional airsheds. Adding 180 gigawatts of US gas turbines locks in decades of fuel combustion and pipeline buildouts. In China, running high-density clusters on power grids backed by 50% coal increases sulfur dioxide and carbon intensity. Although hyperscalers buy off-site solar and wind, hourly matching requirements remain largely unmet, forcing utilities to burn gas and coal during peak computing hours.

### Context

The global data centre fleet currently totals 120 gigawatts, with 80% concentrated in the United States, China, and the European Union. Under the IEA central scenario detailed in its [data centres tracking program](https://www.iea.org/energy-system/buildings/data-centres-and-data-transmission-networks), global capacity will double to 240 gigawatts by 2030, with AI representing 75% of the expansion.

This buildout also carries severe public health and pollution consequences. Earlier this month, researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology documented how data centre emissions propagate hundreds of miles across regional power pools, as reported on [AI Usage Global](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/mit-study-links-5-3-of-us-power-deaths-to-data-center-emissions). The new findings demonstrate that localized pollution spikes reflect a coordinated international shift toward fossil generation.

## Risks and open questions

The forecast hinges on hardware availability and whether utilities can complete planned gas interconnections amid supply chain backlogs for large turbines and transformers. Local pushback and state utility commission scrutiny have already delayed several behind-the-meter gas projects across Texas and Virginia. It remains uncertain whether federal regulators will permit pipeline expansions at the pace hyperscalers demand, or if rising gas prices will alter computing economics.

Additionally, the IEA figures do not fully account for potential efficiency gains from optical interconnects or specialized low-power inference chips, which could soften overall demand if adopted at scale.

## What happens next

National delegations and utility regulators will review the IEA findings ahead of upcoming COP planning sessions and domestic grid reliability proceedings. In the United States, the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission continues evaluating large-load co-location tariffs and grid cost allocation rules, with formal decisions expected in early 2027. Developers of the 93 tracked AI facilities will advance construction milestones through 2027 and 2028, testing whether regional grids can supply promised interconnection volumes without firing mothballed fossil assets.
