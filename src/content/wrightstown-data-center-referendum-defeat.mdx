## Wisconsin Village Rejects Utility Support for Giant AI Data Center

### Wrightstown residents vote 87% against municipal data center infrastructure commitments

In a decisive local referendum, voters in the village of Wrightstown, Wisconsin, have overwhelmingly rejected a proposal to authorize municipal utility infrastructure and public service commitments for a potential large-scale data center. The non-binding advisory question, held alongside partisan primary elections, saw an overwhelming majority of residents vote against aiding the infrastructure demands of the data center developer.

## Key details

On August 11, 2026, electors in the village of Wrightstown, Wisconsin, voted on a key municipal referendum regarding the development of high-density infrastructure. Out of 1,184 ballots cast on the advisory question, 1,033 voters (87%) voted "no," while only 151 (13%) voted "yes" to advising the Village Board to authorize, support, or facilitate public resources, utility commitments, or capital expenditures to assist a large-scale data center.

The advisory vote was authorized under Wisconsin Statutes § 66.0144 and § 66.0422(3)(b) in response to early discussions between village officials and Cloverleaf Infrastructure. Residents mobilized against the potential development over concerns about the immense electricity, water, and road infrastructure costs that would be required to support the facility, as well as the risk of disrupting the community’s predominantly agricultural character.

### Why this matters

The vote highlights a growing nationwide gap between general support for technological advancement and local willingness to host the physical infrastructure that makes it possible. While a vast majority of the public supports leadership in artificial intelligence, local communities are increasingly refusing to absorb the physical, environmental, and ratepayer impacts of these projects. For developers like Cloverleaf Infrastructure, the 87% rejection demonstrates that securing local power and water supplies is no longer just a technical hurdle, but a major public relations challenge.

### Context

Wrightstown, located in Outagamie and Brown Counties, is a rural village with a deep farming heritage. Cloverleaf Infrastructure had been exploring potential sites for a hyperscale campus in northeast Wisconsin, which is an area increasingly attractive to developers due to regional power grid access. However, following immediate public pushback in early 2026, the Village Board had already enacted a one-year moratorium on data center planning in July 2026. The overwhelming referendum defeat cements the public opposition and sets a strong precedent for other midwestern agricultural communities facing similar speculative infrastructure proposals.

## Risks and open questions

Although the referendum is advisory and non-binding, it presents a major political barrier for local leaders who might otherwise favor the tax revenue generated by industrial development. The primary risk is that proceeding with the project against such an overwhelming public mandate could trigger intense political backlash and litigation. It remains an open question whether Cloverleaf Infrastructure will completely abandon its plans for Wrightstown or seek to address community concerns through alternative, low-impact designs that do not rely on municipal water or grid upgrades.

## What happens next

Following the landslide "no" vote, the Wrightstown Village Board is expected to formalize the rejection of any municipal service agreements with Cloverleaf Infrastructure. The current one-year moratorium on data center projects will remain in place, providing a window for the village to draft and implement much stricter zoning regulations regarding data center noise, energy usage, and water resource management. Cloverleaf and other developers will likely shift their focus to other regional sites where community opposition is less organized or where off-grid power can bypass local municipal utility networks entirely.
