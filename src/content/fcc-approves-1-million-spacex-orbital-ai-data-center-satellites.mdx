## FCC Approves 1 Million SpaceX Orbital AI Data Center Satellites

### US telecom regulator grants SpaceX conditional spectrum license for Starmind low-Earth orbit compute constellation despite debris and astronomy objections

The U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) Space Bureau granted SpaceX conditional authorization on November 25, 2026 to deploy up to 1,000,000 satellites as orbital AI data centers. Under [FCC Order DA 26-1273](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-1273A1.pdf), the agency cleared backup radiofrequency links for the Starmind constellation in low-Earth orbit, issuing the first U.S. regulatory approval for space-based compute infrastructure. SpaceX pitched the system to draw near-continuous solar power and radiate heat into space instead of relying on terrestrial power grids and cooling water.

## Key details

Accepted for filing on February 4, 2026, SpaceX's application (ICFS File No. SAT-LOA-20260108-00016, Call Sign S00798) spans six nominal orbital shell bands between 500 and 1,002 kilometers in altitude at 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations. The 15-year license begins when SpaceX certifies that its first Starmind satellite is operational.

Starmind satellites will route data primarily over optical laser links connected to SpaceX's first- and second-generation Starlink networks. Because of that optical setup, the FCC waived allocation rules so SpaceX can use Ka-band frequencies (18.8–19.3 GHz space-to-Earth and 28.6–29.1 GHz Earth-to-space) strictly for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking, and command on an unprotected, non-interference basis. As [Data Center Dynamics](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/fcc-approves-spacex-application-to-deploy-one-million-satellites-for-data-centers-in-space/) reported, the bureau also waived processing-round and surety bond rules while applying updated deployment milestones.

SpaceX CEO Elon Musk wrote in August 2026 that Starmind "will be essentially an optimized Vera Rubin NVL72 computer," and the firm targets an initial launch in 2027.

### By the numbers

- **1,000,000**: Maximum orbital data center satellites authorized under the Starmind license.
- **500 to 1,002 km**: Nominal operating altitude range across six orbital shell bands.
- **15 years**: License duration once SpaceX certifies its first operational satellite.
- **November 25, 2033**: Deadline for the first operating satellite, followed by 10% (100,000) by November 25, 2035, 50% (500,000) by November 25, 2038, and 100% by November 25, 2040.
- **600 km**: Altitude limit above which SpaceX's orbital debris plan remains deferred pending new filings.

### Why this matters

Ground-based AI campuses face multi-year grid interconnection queues, local water permitting disputes, and rising power bills. In its filing, SpaceX argued that near-constant orbital solar power cuts operating expenses and environmental burdens compared with terrestrial facilities. A July 2026 U.S. Government Accountability Office review cited by [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/27/astronomer-decision-spacex-datacenters) similarly noted that orbital data centers could reduce Earth-bound land, electricity, and water use, while flagging engineering and economic barriers.

Launching hundreds of thousands of compute payloads still consumes propellant and produces atmospheric emissions, because scaling Starmind depends on SpaceX's heavy-lift Starship rocket, which remains in development. Critics also warned of pollution from frequent launches and spacecraft reentries.

### Context

SpaceX's authorization dwarfs earlier orbital compute tests. Google scheduled a 1-kilowatt solar prototype carrying four Trillium TPUs under [Project Suncatcher](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/project-suncatcher-google-to-launch-first-space-data-center-test-in-orbit-next-week), and China approved its state-backed [Space Computing Industry Innovation Center](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/china-unveils-space-computing-center-for-grid-free-ai) in early June 2026.

## Risks and open questions

The FCC granted SpaceX's request only in part and deferred a waiver of the five-year atmospheric reentry disposal rule for satellites ending missions below 2,000 km. SpaceX wants flexibility to move satellites above 600 km into Earth disposal or heliocentric orbits, and the bureau found insufficient evidence to approve that plan yet.

Regulators denied petitions to deny from Amazon's Kuiper Systems, Viasat, and the American Astronomical Society (AAS). Citing an October 2026 rule change, the FCC held that space-based operations fall outside National Environmental Policy Act review and left rocket launch emissions to the Federal Aviation Administration. It also declined to regulate night-sky brightness or infrared waste heat from up to one million satellites as outside its spectrum mandate. AAS said it was "disheartened and dismayed" by the decision.

As [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/25/fcc-approves-spacex-plan-to-put-ai-data-centers-in-space/) reported, Amazon dismissed the filing as a "lofty ambition rather than a real plan" that still "risks real harm." The FCC Order omits total power capacity in megawatts, per-satellite chip draw, and launch-emission figures.

## What happens next

SpaceX has 30 days from November 25, 2026 to decline the conditioned license before acceptance takes effect. Before operating above 600 km or using Ka-band ground links, it must submit a revised orbital debris plan, obtain gateway earth station licenses, and finish NTIA coordination with federal agencies. Semi-annual reports starting January 1 and July 1 must track conjunction maneuvers, active satellites, Ka-band use, and reentries ahead of the November 25, 2033 launch deadline.
