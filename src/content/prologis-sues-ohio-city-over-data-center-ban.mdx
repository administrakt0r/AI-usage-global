## Prologis Sues Ohio City Over Retroactive 25MW Data Center Ban

### Developer fights to protect a 250MW AWS campus after 80% voter ballot approval halts large power facilities

Commercial real estate developer Prologis has filed a federal lawsuit against the city of Trenton, Ohio, challenging a voter-approved charter amendment that bans data centers requiring more than 25 megawatts of electrical capacity. The legal challenge seeks to protect Project Mila, an authorized 250-megawatt hyperscale data center campus reportedly designed for Amazon Web Services that is already under active construction. The conflict highlights an intensifying legal battleground between hyperscale cloud operators securing massive power allocations and local communities mobilizing ballot referendums to shield municipal grids from exponential artificial intelligence energy demands.

## Key details

In October 2025, Prologis acquired 141 acres within the Trenton Industrial Park from the City of Trenton for $7.7 million, located in Butler County approximately 30 miles north of downtown Cincinnati. Project Mila was designed as a four-building hyperscale computing campus comprising 880,000 square feet—consisting of four single-story facilities of 220,000 square feet each—supported by a dedicated on-site electrical substation. The development is engineered to consume up to 250 megawatts of continuous electrical power, primarily to host Amazon Web Services cloud and artificial intelligence infrastructure.

The Trenton Planning Commission granted unanimous site plan approval in March 2026, and municipal authorities issued corresponding zoning certificates and building permits. Prologis began earthmoving operations, relocating roughly 325,000 cubic yards of soil, installing underground grounding arrays, and completing extensive subterranean utility trenches, committing more than $42 million in capital expenditures to the site.

On November 3, 2026, approximately 80 percent of Trenton voters approved Issue 17, an amendment to the municipal charter prohibiting the development and construction of any data center facility requiring 25 megawatts or more of electrical power. On November 12, 2026, Prologis filed a civil action in the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Ohio against the City of Trenton. The lawsuit asserts that applying Issue 17 retroactively to an already-approved, heavily capitalized development violates federal constitutional protections, and requests declaratory and injunctive relief to prevent the city from revoking existing building permits or blocking site construction.

Simultaneously, local taxpayer and environmental coalitions, including Conserve Ohio and Woodsdale and Trenton Environmental Resistance (W.A.T.E.R.), submitted formal notices to Trenton municipal leadership demanding immediate revocation of Prologis's zoning certificate, warning they will sue the municipality if officials fail to enforce the voter-approved charter mandate. Conserve Ohio is also organizing a statewide initiative to place a uniform 25 MW data center cap into the Ohio state constitution.

### Why this matters

The computing capacity required for training frontier AI models and sustaining inference at scale is forcing data center developers to aggregate hundreds of megawatts of electricity per facility. In regions like southwestern Ohio, these concentrated electrical loads compete directly with local manufacturing and residential grid allocations, driving up regional capacity costs and heightening grid congestion.

The Trenton lawsuit establishes a precedent-setting legal test over whether municipal voters can use direct democracy to halt hyperscale data centers after initial local zoning approvals have been issued. If municipal power bans are upheld retroactively, tech companies and infrastructure developers face heightened capital risks, potentially requiring higher risk premiums and longer timelines for AI infrastructure buildouts. Conversely, if federal courts rule that municipalities cannot enforce retroactive bans once initial permits are issued, communities may move to preemptively enact moratoriums before developers can even acquire land or submit preliminary site plans.

### Context

Ohio has emerged as one of the primary corridors for hyperscale data center expansion in North America, with major cloud providers investing tens of billions of dollars across Butler, Franklin, and Licking counties. However, this growth relies heavily on power supplied through the PJM Interconnection grid, where data center load growth has fueled concerns over rising retail electricity bills and extended interconnection delays.

A recent study published in Nature Sustainability by researchers at Harvard University found that hyperscale data centers in the United States emitted 25.7 million metric tons of CO2 annually between mid-2024 and mid-2025, with just four states—Ohio, Virginia, Oregon, and Iowa—accounting for more than half of that electricity consumption. Furthermore, over 54 percent of that power was generated by fossil fuels, including 38 percent from natural gas. The clash in Trenton mirrors similar local pushbacks across the country, where communities are resisting the water consumption and power demands of massive computing hubs.

## Risks and open questions

The immediate legal question before the Southern District of Ohio is whether Prologis holds vested property rights that insulate Project Mila from subsequently enacted charter amendments. A prolonged injunction battle could leave the 141-acre site idle while legal fees and carrying costs mount for both the developer and the city.

If the city of Trenton is forced to revoke permits under pressure from citizen lawsuits, Prologis could file takings and damages claims seeking to recover the more than $42 million already expended, exposing the municipality to significant financial liability. For regional grid planners, the unpredictable timing of municipal approvals and local referendums introduces severe forecasting volatility. When a planned 250 MW load faces sudden legal challenges, utility transmission planning must balance the risk of costly overbuilding against the potential for localized grid strain.

## What happens next

The U.S. District Court for the Southern District of Ohio will review Prologis’s petition for injunctive relief and determine whether construction activities may proceed while constitutional claims are litigated. Trenton City Council must formally decide how to respond to competing litigation threats from Prologis and community advocates, determining whether to defend the ballot measure in federal court or explore an administrative resolution.

Advocacy groups led by Conserve Ohio will continue collecting signatures to place a constitutional amendment on the Ohio ballot, seeking to codify a statewide 25 MW threshold that would prevent any future hyperscale data center development across Ohio without explicit legislative authorization.
