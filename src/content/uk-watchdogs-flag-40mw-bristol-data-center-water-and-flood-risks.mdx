## UK Watchdogs Flag 40MW Bristol Data Center Water and Flood Risks

### Natural England and the Environment Agency urged Bristol City Council to reject the Avonmouth proposal before ministers floated scrapping both regulators

Natural England has urged Bristol City Council to refuse planning permission for a proposed 40MW AI data center at Avonmouth, citing wildlife disturbance risks 600 meters from the Severn Estuary Special Protection Area. The Environment Agency also objected over high flood risk and missing water-use figures, weeks before UK ministers threatened to abolish both independent watchdogs.

## Key details

Developer Rivington Energy wants to build the 40MW facility in Avonmouth, an industrial port district near Bristol. In advice submitted earlier this month, Natural England warned that the project could cause "disturbance and displacement" to rare and vulnerable migratory birds using the Severn Estuary Special Protection Area, 600 meters from the site.

Natural England also noted that the developer omitted Hallen Marsh, a protected site 270 meters away, from its noise pollution screening. The Environment Agency lodged a separate objection, warning that the plot sits on a high-risk floodplain where staff might lack safe refuge during severe flooding, and that Rivington Energy provided no figures on water demand or safeguards against contaminated runoff entering controlled waters.

More than 4,000 people have objected to the Avonmouth application. Bristol City Council ruled that the proposal is "likely to give rise to significant environmental effects" and required a full environmental impact assessment.

### By the numbers

- **40MW**: Proposed capacity of the Avonmouth AI data center
- **600 meters**: Distance from the site to the Severn Estuary Special Protection Area for migratory birds
- **270 meters**: Distance to Hallen Marsh, a protected wetland omitted from initial noise screening
- **4,000+**: Public objections filed against the Bristol development
- **73GW**: Combined grid connection demand across more than 300 UK data center projects in the queue

### Why this matters

In an [October 2026 Habitats Regulations Assessment](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6ae1cc73088f42d99bbe77d3/draft-national-policy-statement-for-data-centres-habitats-regulations-assessment.pdf) for the UK government's draft National Policy Statement for data centers, Natural England warned that data centers are "a relatively new development type" whose full ecological effects remain poorly understood. It highlighted "the high volumes of water potentially required for data centre cooling operations" and called for safeguards against river, stream, lake, and wetland overabstraction, thermal discharges, and fish impingement or entrainment across England's 90 Special Protection Areas, 256 Special Areas of Conservation, and 73 Ramsar sites.

Local councils depend on statutory agencies to enforce those disclosures before construction begins. When a developer omits water volumes and flood-refuge engineering from an initial filing, independent objections force formal environmental impact assessments that local planning teams often lack the specialist staff to conduct alone.

### Context

Britain's electricity queue already reflects acute competition for power and land. More than 300 planned AI data centers are waiting for grid connections totaling 73GW, nearly double the UK's peak winter load. That grid backlog recently pushed [Microsoft to abandon a 90MW AI supercomputer campus in Essex](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/microsoft-abandons-uk-ai-supercomputer-over-power-grid-delays) after network upgrades slipped into the 2030s.

To sidestep local refusals, UK ministers are letting data centers of 100MW or more apply through the Nationally Significant Infrastructure Projects regime, making a cabinet secretary the final decision-maker. On November 23, de facto deputy prime minister Louise Haigh told business leaders the government is reviewing whether to dissolve or devolve functions from Natural England, the Environment Agency, Ofwat, and the Drinking Water Inspectorate, blaming [more than 20 statutory consultees for delaying construction](https://www.theguardian.com/politics/2026/nov/23/natural-england-could-be-abolished-quango-cull-louise-haigh).

## Risks and open questions

Government sources denied that the Avonmouth objection or Natural England's stance on data centers prompted Haigh's review. Still, Wildlife Trusts chief executive Craig Bennett warned that dismantling arm's-length regulators would replace science-based oversight with ministerial discretion, and Friends of the Earth AI campaigner Mary Stevens questioned whether ministers would protect habitats independently.

Rivington Energy has still not disclosed Avonmouth's projected cooling water consumption, runoff controls, or flood-refuge design. It is also unclear whether the site would rely on fossil-fuel backup generation while waiting for grid capacity, an approach many UK developers pursue alongside their connection applications.

## What happens next

Rivington Energy said it is preparing additional studies and a detailed environmental statement to answer the Environment Agency, Natural England, and Bristol City Council. Council planners will assess that submission before any planning decision, while UK ministers weigh whether to advance legislation stripping or redistributing the regulators' powers.
