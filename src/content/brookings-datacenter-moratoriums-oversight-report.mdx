## Brookings: AI Data Center Moratoriums Fail Without Real Oversight

### New report tracks pauses across 15 states and over 100 localities

A new report from the Brookings Institution warns that a growing wave of data center moratoriums across the United States is insufficient to address the long-term resource demands of the artificial intelligence boom. While at least 15 states have weighed pauses on data center development and over 100 localities have approved them, the researchers argue that these temporary freezes fail to establish meaningful regulatory oversight. For AI systems to scale sustainably, the report urges policymakers to implement transparent resource auditing and robust ratepayer protections rather than simple construction bans.

## Key details

The rapid build-out of artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure has triggered a massive nationwide backlash from communities concerned about resource depletion. In response, local and state legislators have increasingly turned to construction freezes. According to the Brookings report, at least 15 state legislatures have weighed pauses on data center development, and at least 100 localities have already approved their own moratoriums.

Among state-level actions, New York recently became the first to enact a statewide moratorium through executive order on July 15, 2026, pausing environmental permits for data centers consuming at least 50 megawatts of power for up to one year. Conversely, in Maine, a legislative ban on permitting data centers until November 2027 was vetoed by Governor Janet Mills in April 2026. On the federal level, Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez have introduced the Artificial Intelligence Data Center Moratorium Act, targeting projects with peak power loads exceeding 20 megawatts or using liquid cooling systems. House Energy and Commerce Ranking Member Frank Pallone Jr. has also called for a national moratorium. The report underscores that these pauses are driven by genuine public concerns over residential electricity rate increases, groundwater depletion, and localized air pollution.

### Why this matters

Slowing down physical construction does not alter the underlying, exponential growth in demand for AI computing power. Moratoriums are temporary measures that fail to provide long-term, systematic oversight of data centers. Without transparent auditing and mandatory resource reporting, developers may simply shift their projects to regions with weaker grids or fewer environmental regulations, shuffling the ecological footprint rather than reducing it.

### Context

The generative AI boom has put unprecedented strain on global resources, particularly energy grids and watersheds. High-density AI chips require massive amounts of power and water for cooling, with large facilities using up to 5 million gallons of water daily. In 2025, large data center developers accounted for 72% of corporate clean power procurement via power purchase agreements (PPAs) in the Americas. This immense resource draw has led to a trust deficit, fueling nationwide protests, such as a major demonstration in Imperial, California on July 18, 2026.

## Risks and open questions

One major risk is that broad, poorly drafted moratoriums could stall technological innovation and cause significant financial distress for digital infrastructure companies. Additionally, because public officials currently lack access to proprietary, granular data regarding actual data center resource efficiency, creating effective, evidence-based regulation remains extremely difficult. It remains to be seen whether voluntary pledges by tech giants to fund grid upgrades will protect household utility bills from rising.

## What happens next

In states with active moratoriums, such as New York, environmental agencies will use the pauses to evaluate grid impacts and draft stricter guidelines for energy and water conservation. Nationally, pressure will grow to pass durable ratepayer protections, such as the Ratepayer Protection Act which recently advanced in the U.S. House of Representatives. Tech companies will likely face increasing demands to negotiate community benefit agreements and fund local infrastructure improvements before receiving construction permits.
