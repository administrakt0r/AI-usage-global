## Alberta Mandates Near-Zero Water Cooling for 10MW Data Centres

### Proposed Bill 42 establishes strict cooling standards, 600-meter setbacks, and grid protections across 25GW in connection requests

The Government of Alberta has introduced legislation requiring data centers of 10 megawatts or larger to slash cooling water use close to zero, enforce 600-meter residential setbacks, and fund their own grid infrastructure. Tabled as Bill 42, the Energy, Environment and Utilities Statutes Amendment Act, 2026, the measures arrive as the provincial grid operator faces over 25,000 megawatts in interconnection requests—nearly double Alberta's record peak demand of 12,800 megawatts. Provincial officials estimate the rules will directly impact roughly 30 percent of proposed data center developments in the regional pipeline.

## Key details

Under the proposed statute announced by [Technology and Innovation Minister Nate Glubish](https://www.alberta.ca/protecting-land-and-communities), data centers drawing 10 megawatts (MW) or more will fall under direct oversight of the Alberta Utilities Commission (AUC). The legislation creates a statutory buffer of 600 meters—equivalent to roughly 10 standard NHL rinks laid end to end—between qualifying facilities and the nearest home. While municipalities may request alternative setbacks for standalone facilities under 75 MW that lack on-site generation, all larger campuses must observe the provincial buffer.

To protect agricultural land, Bill 42 bans large computing facilities from more than 14 million acres of prime farmland. This includes 10.2 million acres of Class 1 and 2 soils, 3.5 million acres of Class 3 land in 14 municipalities without higher-grade soil, and 1.8 million acres of irrigated farmland. The bill also directs regulators to establish binding cooling standards requiring operators to bring consumptive water use close to zero while capturing waste heat for greenhouses, district heating, or local industry.

To protect household electricity bills, the bill creates a Data Centre Designation requiring developers to pay 100 percent of dedicated transmission and substation costs. As reported by [Water Canada](https://www.watercanada.net/alberta-proposes-new-restrictions-on-data-centres/), the province is raising maximum corporate fines for serious environmental contamination from $1 million to $6 million, while requiring data centers to curtail electricity first during grid emergencies before residents face outages.

### By the numbers

- **25,000 MW**: Data center interconnection requests submitted to the Alberta Electric System Operator
- **12,800 MW**: Alberta's historical all-time peak electrical demand across all sectors
- **10 MW**: Capacity threshold triggering Alberta Utilities Commission oversight and statutory limits
- **600 meters**: Minimum legal buffer distance required between commercial computing facilities and homes
- **14 million acres**: Agricultural and irrigated land placed off-limits to large computing facilities
- **$6 million**: Proposed statutory maximum corporate fine for serious environmental contamination

### Why this matters

The rapid expansion of AI training clusters has intensified competition for regional power and water supplies. In municipalities like Sturgeon County and Rocky View County, residents have raised alarms over potential electricity rate increases, groundwater depletion, and industrial noise. Earlier reporting on [AI Usage Global](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/canada-faces-surging-ai-data-center-pipeline-and-growing-backlash) showed Canadian municipalities struggling to manage developer land and power requests.

By designating the AUC as primary regulator and enforcing uniform water benchmarks, Alberta aims to prevent local zoning disputes while protecting ratepayers. Mandating near-zero cooling water is especially vital in southern Alberta, where recent droughts have placed municipal water supplies and irrigation networks under heavy strain.

### Context

Alberta has become a leading Canadian destination for hyperscale developers due to its deregulated power market and cool northern climate. Over 60 conventional facilities operate across the province, but hyperscale demands have accelerated rapidly. While Meta secured approvals for a $13 billion campus in Sturgeon County before the bill, other proposals—such as a 1.4 GW gas-fired project in Olds, Alberta—were rejected by regulators over community proximity concerns.

The policy shift mirrors broader efforts across North America to manage data center impacts. As highlighted in research on [data center water consumption](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/bell-canada-1-2gw-ai-campus-masks-heavy-indirect-water-demand), unconstrained evaporative cooling can withdraw millions of liters of water daily during peak summer operations. Alberta's framework represents one of the first Canadian provincial laws combining agricultural bans, near-zero water standards, and full ratepayer insulation into a single statute.

## Risks and open questions

The legislation grandfathers projects that already hold municipal permits, AUC approvals, or executed transmission contracts. Opposition lawmakers have argued that exempting previously approved facilities leaves substantial grid and water demands unaddressed.

Questions also remain regarding technical performance. Transitioning large AI clusters to dry cooling can increase parasitic electrical loads during summer heat waves. Regulators have yet to finalize rules detailing how near-zero water compliance will be verified across rural installations.

## What happens next

Bill 42 will proceed through readings in the Legislative Assembly before royal assent. The Alberta Utilities Commission and environmental regulators will then begin public rule-making to finalize cooling water thresholds and waste heat recovery standards ahead of implementation in 2027.
