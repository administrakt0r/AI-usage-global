## MIT Study Links 5.3% of US Power Deaths to Data Center Emissions

### Research in Environmental Research Letters traces 650 annual deaths to grid strain and fossil generation

Electricity demanded by U.S. data centers caused approximately 650 premature deaths in 2023, accounting for 5.3% of all power-sector air quality mortality nationwide, according to a study by MIT researchers. The peer-reviewed findings reveal that air pollution from data center electricity demand disproportionately harms rural communities in the Midwest, far from major server hubs in Virginia and Texas.

## Key details

The study, published in [Environmental Research Letters](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/aebbd4) by researchers from MIT, the University of California at Irvine, and Yale University, evaluated unit-level dispatch across more than 10,000 power generators in the contiguous United States. To trace health impacts, the team coupled the United States Electricity Generation Optimization grid model with the GEOS-Chem atmospheric chemical transport model.

By comparing hourly dispatch with and without data center loads throughout 2023, the researchers found that meeting continuous 24/7 electricity demand forced utilities to dispatch marginal generation from older coal and natural gas units. These plants emit sulfur dioxide (SO2) and nitrogen oxides (NOx), which react in the atmosphere to form fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ground-level ozone.

The model estimates that data center electricity caused 513 premature deaths from PM2.5 and 137 deaths from ozone in 2023. While power demand was heavily concentrated in Northern Virginia and Texas, the highest per-capita mortality rates occurred in downwind states: Iowa, Nevada, South Dakota, Kansas, and Nebraska. Per-capita mortality rates in Virginia and Texas were roughly half those of several Midwestern states.

### By the numbers

- **650**: estimated annual premature U.S. deaths from data center electricity emissions
- **5.3%**: share of total U.S. electricity-generation mortality attributable to data centers
- **513**: premature deaths caused by fine particulate matter (PM2.5)
- **137**: premature deaths caused by ground-level ozone
- **4.0%**: data centers' estimated share of total U.S. electricity demand in 2023

### Why this matters

The findings challenge assumptions that data center impacts are confined to host communities. Because electrical grids are interconnected, electricity demanded by server clusters in one region frequently ramps up coal and gas plants hundreds of miles away in the Ohio Valley.

"When people think about the negative impacts of data centers, they usually focus on the neighbors," said senior author Noelle Selin, a professor in the MIT Institute for Data, Systems, and Society. "Even if you don’t think you are affected because there is not a data center near you, think again."

When constant computing loads connect to regional grids, operators dispatch flexible marginal units to balance supply. "Different regions have different mixes of power," Selin said. "If you turn on a major new demand source, by plugging a data center into the grid, you might have to fire up your really dirty power plant to meet that new demand."

### Context

Data centers represented roughly 4% of total U.S. electricity demand and 4.4% of national emissions in 2023, according to estimates from the [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://eta.lbl.gov/). An earlier analysis on [AI Usage Global](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/harvard-study-us-hyperscale-ai-data-center-emissions) found that hyperscale campuses draw more than half their electricity from fossil fuels.

The MIT paper is the first to connect hourly marginal grid dispatch to atmospheric chemistry and epidemiologically validated mortality. In the Midwest, atmospheric ammonia from agriculture combines with power plant sulfur and nitrogen emissions, accelerating PM2.5 formation and multiplying health risks in farming communities that host little computing infrastructure.

## Risks and open questions

The study analyzed grid-supplied power in 2023 and does not model emissions from diesel backup generators or behind-the-meter natural gas turbines increasingly deployed at newer AI facilities. If tech firms install localized fossil generation to bypass utility connection delays, local air pollution could increase substantially.

The authors also assumed steady around-the-clock power consumption. Whether flexible scheduling of AI training workloads could shift demand to cleaner hours remains unproven at scale.

## What happens next

The research team plans to evaluate emissions from on-site generation and assess whether flexible computing loads can mitigate pollution. Lead author Yuang Chen noted that siting decisions must look beyond energy costs: "When people are thinking about data center siting and operations, it is important to consider more than just things like land and energy prices. They should also take air quality considerations into the equation."

Regional grid operators including PJM and MISO are reviewing large-load tariffs to ensure data center developers fund clean capacity rather than straining existing generation assets.
