## Ohio Approves 800MW Data Center Gas Plant Despite Voter Rejection

### Regulators issue a siting certificate for EdgeConneX's behind-the-meter generation campus weeks after 81% of Ashville village voters opposed the project.

The Ohio Power Siting Board has approved an 800-megawatt natural gas power plant designed exclusively to supply an adjacent hyperscale data center campus in Ashville, Ohio. The November 19 certificate clears developer EdgeConneX to build dedicated behind-the-meter generation despite an 81% voter referendum rejecting the municipality's agreement with the company. The decision accelerates a growing trend where data center developers bypass congested utility interconnection queues by building captive fossil-fuel generation directly on site.

## Key details

The 108-page order issued in [Case No. 26-196-EL-BGN](https://opsb.ohio.gov) grants PowerConneX, an affiliate of developer EdgeConneX, authority to construct the Ashville Energy Center across 110 acres in Pickaway County, approximately 20 miles south of Columbus. According to reporting by [Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/data-centers/ohio-oks-gas-plant-for-data-centers), the facility will deploy reciprocating internal combustion engines and simple-cycle natural gas combustion turbines to deliver up to 800 MW of dedicated electricity.

Unlike conventional power stations that feed wholesale electricity into the regional grid, the Ashville plant operates behind the meter. The power generated flows solely into an adjacent data center campus rather than the transmission lines operated by PJM Interconnection. EdgeConneX pursued dedicated generation because grid connection studies across central Ohio now face multi-year backlogs.

The approval follows intense local friction. In the November 3 general election, 81% of Ashville voters cast ballots against a village council resolution endorsing the project, even after the developer agreed to provide up to $102 million in local municipal and school district contributions. In its final ruling, the Ohio Power Siting Board determined that its statutory siting jurisdiction supersedes municipal referendums. Regulators concluded the project serves the public interest, citing economic output and 41 binding permit conditions covering noise attenuation, operating hours, and landscaping buffers.

### By the numbers

- **800 MW**: Maximum nameplate generating capacity of the captive natural gas plant.
- **81%**: Share of Ashville village voters who rejected the municipal development agreement on November 3.
- **110 acres**: Land parcel designated for the generation facility along State Route 752.
- **$102 million**: Total value of community and educational contribution commitments offered by the developer.
- **41**: Environmental, construction, and operational compliance conditions imposed by the state board.
- **108 pages**: Length of the regulatory order approving the certificate of environmental compatibility and public need.

### Why this matters

The Ashville approval illustrates how artificial intelligence workloads are reshaping electricity generation. Faced with five- to seven-year delays to interconnect to regional transmission networks, hyperscalers and wholesale developers are turning to "bring-your-own-power" architectures. By placing reciprocating gas engines directly adjacent to data halls, operators secure immediate baseload power without waiting for utility transmission upgrades.

This approach locks in long-term fossil fuel consumption. Simple-cycle turbines and reciprocating gas engines emit substantial nitrogen oxides, volatile organic compounds, and carbon dioxide. Because the generation sits within hundreds of yards of local homes, soccer fields, and a public park, the emissions fall directly on nearby residents rather than being dispersed through regional utility stacks located far from residential centers.

### Context

Clean energy organizations and local officials criticized the board's decision as a double standard. According to the Ohio Environmental Council, the Power Siting Board had previously rejected renewable energy projects—including the Grange Solar project in Logan County and the Crossroads Solar facility in Morrow County—citing unified local community opposition. In Ashville, regulators approved fossil generation despite documented voter rejection at the ballot box.

The conflict in Pickaway County mirrors mounting resistance across the PJM footprint. Ohio communities have increasingly moved to curb data center expansions; earlier this month, [voters in Trenton, Ohio passed a charter amendment capping data center power at 25 MW](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/prologis-sues-ohio-city-over-data-center-ban), prompting a federal lawsuit from developer Prologis. Meanwhile, [seven mid-Atlantic states requested delays in PJM capacity auctions](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/seven-states-urge-pjm-to-delay-capacity-auction-over-10b-data-center-forecasts) after surging data center forecasts drove billions in projected grid costs. At a global level, the International Energy Agency recently projected that [data center carbon emissions will surge 70% by 2030](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/iea-projects-70-surge-in-data-center-carbon-emissions-by-2030) as tech firms turn to natural gas and coal to feed computing loads.

## Risks and open questions

The siting certificate does not conclude the regulatory hurdles facing the Ashville Energy Center. PowerConneX must still secure air pollution permits from the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency. During hearings, intervening parties noted that the developer selected simple-cycle turbines and reciprocating engines instead of cleaner combined-cycle units or hydrogen-ready fuel cells due to upfront capital costs and equipment delivery timelines. State air regulators must determine whether the projected emissions meet National Ambient Air Quality Standards.

Local water consumption also remains an open issue. While simple-cycle gas turbines require less cooling water than steam plants, cooling the co-located data halls will require water resources from Pickaway County aquifers. Opponents questioned whether local municipal water treatment and pumping infrastructure can handle the combined load during summer peak periods without affecting residential supplies.

## What happens next

Opponents and intervening groups have 30 days from the November 19 order to file applications for rehearing with the Ohio Power Siting Board. If the board denies the requests or refuses to alter its decision, opponents can appeal directly to the Ohio Supreme Court. In parallel, the Ohio EPA will conduct public review periods on the facility's draft air and water permits. Construction cannot begin until all state environmental permits are formally issued.
