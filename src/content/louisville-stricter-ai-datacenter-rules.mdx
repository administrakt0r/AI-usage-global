## Louisville Proposes Stricter AI Data Center Zoning Rules

### Updated proposal adds 500-foot setback and restricts backup generator use after 3,600 comments

The Louisville Metro Office of Planning has recommended significantly tighter zoning regulations for future data center developments in the city. The updated draft zoning rules follow a 30-day public comment period during which the city received more than 3,600 comments from concerned residents and stakeholders. These new restrictions aim to protect neighborhoods, municipal infrastructure, and utility ratepayers from the massive resource footprint associated with high-density artificial intelligence computing.

## Key details

The revised proposal, released on July 24, 2026, retains the core ban on hyperscale data centers larger than 500,000 square feet first proposed in June. However, after extensive public feedback, the city's Office of Planning added specific and strict environmental and neighborhood safeguards to the text amendment of the Land Development Code (LDC):

- **Stricter Setbacks:** Large-scale data centers must be built at least 500 feet away from "sensitive uses," which include existing homes, hospitals, nursing homes, childcare centers, and schools. Medium-scale facilities (between 50,000 and 250,000 square feet) must maintain a 200-foot setback.
- **District Restrictions:** Data centers larger than 250,000 square feet are restricted exclusively to industrial areas in the M-3 district and require a Conditional Use Permit. Facilities between 50,000 and 250,000 square feet are restricted to EZ-1, M-2, M-3, and PEC districts. Smaller facilities under 50,000 square feet are permitted only in light manufacturing or industrial zones.
- **Generator and Turbine Limits:** Backup generator testing is restricted to once per week between the hours of 8 a.m. and 5 p.m., and regular use is prohibited outside of emergencies. Crucially, the draft clarifies that co-located power plants and gas turbines are not allowed as part of data center operations.
- **Accessory Loophole Closed:** To prevent developers from building massive facilities under the guise of secondary uses, accessory data centers are now capped at the lesser of 25,000 square feet or 49.9% of the building's total floor area.
- **Ratepayer Protection:** Developers must provide concrete evidence that their facilities will not shift infrastructure or energy costs to residential utility customers, and they must bear the full cost of any required grid or utility upgrades.

### Why this matters

As artificial intelligence models grow in size, the infrastructure required to train and run them consumes vast amounts of electricity and cooling water. By prohibiting co-located gas turbine power plants and banning hyperscale facilities entirely, Louisville is preventing AI developers from bypassing clean energy standards or placing undue strain on the local electrical grid. The requirement for developers to fund their own infrastructure upgrades and prove zero cost-shifting to residential ratepayers sets a strong regulatory precedent for municipal protection against AI-driven utility inflation.

### Context

Louisville's zoning updates are part of a rapidly growing wave of local resistance to AI's physical footprint across North America. Over the past year, cities like Spokane, Washington, and Reno, Nevada, have enacted emergency data center moratoriums, while states like Oregon and Pennsylvania have moved to repeal tax incentives and implement rate hikes. These localized municipal interventions are filling the regulatory void left by federal agencies, such as the US Environmental Protection Agency, which recently declined to establish national resource standards for the data center industry.

## What happens next

The Louisville Metro Planning Commission has scheduled four public hearings to gather additional feedback on the revised Land Development Code amendments. The first hearing is scheduled for Monday, July 27, 2026, at 5 p.m. at Jefferson Community and Technical College (JCTC) Southwest, followed by sessions on August 5, August 6 (online), and August 10. Following these hearings, the Planning Commission will present its final recommendation to the Metro Council and other local legislative bodies for formal approval.
