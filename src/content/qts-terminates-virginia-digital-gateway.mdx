## QTS Terminates $30 Billion Digital Gateway Data Center in Virginia

### Community opposition and permitting delays end plans for 2,139-acre campus

QTS Data Centers has officially withdrawn its appeals for the Digital Gateway project in Prince William County, effectively terminating a proposed $30 billion infrastructure hub. The decision marks a significant reversal for what was intended to be one of the largest AI data center corridors in the United States.

## Key details

The Digital Gateway project was envisioned as a 2,139-acre technology corridor in Northern Virginia, capable of supporting up to 27 million square feet of data center development. QTS and other proponents had projected the project would attract nearly $30 billion in private investment to accommodate the surging demand for AI and cloud computing capacity.

However, the project faced years of sustained opposition from local residents and environmental groups concerned about land use, noise, and the massive power and water requirements of the facility. By withdrawing its legal appeals before the Virginia Supreme Court late last week, QTS has effectively ended the county’s vision for the massive campus. The move highlights how permitting certainty and local resistance are becoming as critical as power availability in determining the success of hyperscale AI projects.

### Why this matters

The cancellation of a $30 billion project demonstrates that even in "Data Center Alley," infrastructure expansion is hitting a wall of social and regulatory resistance. As AI companies race to build larger campuses to support next-generation models, the loss of over 2,000 acres of potential development space will likely tighten global capacity and force developers to seek even more remote or unconventional locations.

### Context

Northern Virginia remains the world's largest data center market, but it is increasingly constrained by grid capacity and public sentiment. The Digital Gateway was seen as the next major frontier for expansion beyond the saturated Loudoun County. Its failure follows a series of similar setbacks for major tech firms in the region, signaling a shift where local political and legal hurdles may now rival electricity shortages as the primary bottleneck for AI infrastructure.

## What happens next

QTS has reaffirmed that Virginia remains a "core part" of its strategy, suggesting the company will pivot toward smaller or less controversial sites within the state. Meanwhile, the termination of the Digital Gateway will likely embolden community activists in other regions, potentially leading to more stringent zoning laws and longer environmental review processes for AI infrastructure nationwide.
