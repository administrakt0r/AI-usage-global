## Providence Reaffirms 100% Data Center Ban and Orders Grid Review

### Mayor Brett Smiley signs Executive Order 2026-05 to block unvetted AI compute facilities and study power, water, and ratepayer burdens.

Providence Mayor Brett Smiley signed Executive Order 2026-05 on November 23, 2026, confirming that commercial data centers remain 100% prohibited across all municipal zoning districts. The directive establishes an inter-departmental task force to evaluate how AI and cloud infrastructure impact local electric distribution, municipal water supplies, and resident utility bills before the city considers any zoning amendments.

## Key details

Under Providence's existing zoning ordinance, data centers are not listed as an allowable principal use anywhere in the city. The executive action formalizes that restriction while directing the Department of Planning and Development, alongside the Sustainability Department, to draft a comprehensive municipal policy. The administration announced the measure with City Councilmember Oscar Vargas, Rhode Island League of Cities and Towns Executive Director Randy Rossi, and labor leaders from the Rhode Island AFL-CIO.

The review instructs municipal planners to examine server campus scale, define high-density compute facilities, and quantify environmental externalities:

- Substation capacity limits and peak electricity demand across urban neighborhoods.
- Cooling water consumption and wastewater discharge volumes relative to municipal water authority capacity.
- Continuous chiller noise and air pollution from backup diesel generator tests.
- Spillover impacts on residential electric bills if regional transmission operators pass grid upgrade costs to local consumers.

Planners will also coordinate with state agencies and lawmakers. Mayor Smiley warned that without uniform state rules, facilities built in neighboring towns could drive up transmission charges for urban households that receive no local tax benefits.

### By the numbers

- **100%**: Share of municipal zoning districts in Providence where data centers are prohibited as a principal use.
- **2026-05**: Number of the executive order directing city planners to draft formal data center standards.
- **15**: Wards in Providence whose neighborhoods and industrial buffer zones fall under the review.
- **500 kW**: Typical capacity threshold where server installations shift from standard commercial loads to intensive industrial utilities.

### Why this matters

Data centers built for artificial intelligence require multi-megawatt grid interconnections and millions of gallons of cooling water, resources that dense coastal cities cannot easily absorb. In Providence, industrial parcels border residential neighborhoods in wards like South Providence and Silver Lake. Siting unvetted server warehouses on those parcels risks noise complaints and degrades urban air quality through localized diesel exhaust.

Beyond physical zoning, municipal leaders are responding to regional economic pressure. In wholesale power markets across the Northeast, rapid computing growth has driven sharp increases in transmission and capacity rates. Because transmission costs are shared across utility customer classes, residential consumers face higher electric bills even if local governments reject server facilities inside their own borders.

### Context

Providence joins a growing list of municipalities enacting formal barriers to rapid data center expansion. Across the United States, local councils and zoning boards have pushed back against utility strain, echoing disputes seen when [Prologis sued an Ohio municipality over local data center bans](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/prologis-sues-ohio-city-over-retroactive-25mw-data-center-ban). At the federal level, lawmakers have pushed similar cost protections, reflected in the [US Senate passing the BAAJA bill to allocate grid upgrade costs directly to large computational loads](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/us-senate-passes-baaja-bill-requiring-20mw-data-centers-to-fund-grid).

According to the International Energy Agency, data centers consumed roughly 460 TWh of electricity worldwide in 2022, a figure on pace to double by 2026 as generative AI models proliferate. Urban centers with legacy electrical grids face bottleneck risks if operators attempt to convert industrial warehouses without dedicated utility generation.

## Risks and open questions

The executive order does not permanently ban data centers from Providence. Any permanent zoning change, whether to create an industrial overlay district or codify an explicit ban, requires approval from the 15-member Providence City Council, which has not yet scheduled hearings.

Regional coordination also faces political hurdles. While Providence municipal leaders called for statewide standards, the Rhode Island General Assembly has not introduced legislation capping data center water use or mandating off-grid power generation. If neighboring towns offer tax incentives or expedited permits, regional grid operator ISO New England could still dispatch higher-cost generation, raising utility bills statewide.

## What happens next

The Department of Planning and Development will begin drafting policy guidelines and hosting public community input sessions in neighborhoods bordering industrial zones. Planning staff will deliver recommendations to Mayor Smiley and the City Council, outlining whether conditional use permits or strict scale limits should govern future developers.

Concurrently, the administration plans to lobby state lawmakers ahead of the 2027 legislative session to establish statewide ratepayer protections and infrastructure cost-allocation rules.
