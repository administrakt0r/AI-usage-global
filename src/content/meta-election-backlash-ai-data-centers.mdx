## Meta Warns Election Backlash Threatens AI Data Center Growth

### Tech giant acknowledges political pushback over energy and grid demands

Meta has warned that political victories for anti-data-center candidates in recent midterm elections will significantly complicate plans to expand artificial intelligence computing infrastructure. Speaking at the WSJ Tech Live conference, company executives acknowledged that public anxiety over rising electricity rates and power grid strain has turned data centers into prominent political targets.

## Key details

Speaking at the conference, Meta President Dina Powell McCormick stated that recent midterm election outcomes across several states will make building new data centers substantially harder. McCormick noted that political candidates successfully campaigned on anti-data-center platforms, tapping into community frustration over massive utility demands and perceived lack of local economic benefits. She acknowledged that the tech industry has failed to adequately demonstrate the direct advantages of hyperscale investments to affected residents.

Meta currently operates infrastructure across 23 states, including five major campuses designed for 1 gigawatt or more of power capacity, such as its flagship facilities in Texas. To overcome mounting resistance, McCormick outlined strict community deal criteria that Meta now uses to win local approvals. These frameworks require the company to pay for its own power generation and grid interconnection costs, direct data center tax surpluses specifically toward local teachers and first responders, mandate local union labor forces, and secure bipartisan political buy-in prior to construction.

### Why this matters

The pushback against artificial intelligence infrastructure marks a significant shift from passive community acceptance to active electoral intervention. Large-scale training and inference facilities require hundreds of megawatts to gigawatts of baseload electricity alongside millions of gallons of cooling water, frequently competing with residential customers for regional grid capacity. When voters elect officials explicitly committed to halting or conditioning data center developments, hyperscalers face direct permit denials, extended environmental reviews, and rising capital costs to secure dedicated behind-the-meter energy.

### Context

Anti-data-center sentiment has surged across the United States as utility regulators and regional transmission operators confront unprecedented load growth forecasts. In states like Virginia, Texas, Georgia, and Indiana, residential consumers have seen power bills climb as utilities build expensive substations and gas turbines to serve hyperscalers. National polling showed that energy costs and grid reliability were top-of-mind issues in the recent midterms, leading state and municipal leaders to propose moratoria, specialized data center utility rate classes, and bans on local nondisclosure agreements.

## Risks and open questions

A central question is whether voluntary community concessions—such as funding municipal services or funding private power plants—will satisfy skeptical voters and regulators. If states enforce binding statutory moratoria or mandatory local referendum votes, hyperscalers may struggle to bring online the gigawatts of capacity needed to power next-generation foundation models. Additionally, if consumer AI applications fail to generate sufficient commercial revenue to justify hundreds of billions in capital expenditures, tech giants could find themselves holding expensive, stranded infrastructure assets in hostile regulatory jurisdictions.

## What happens next

Meta and other hyperscale cloud providers are expected to intensify their public lobbying campaigns to convince municipal and state governments of the economic value of AI compute clusters. Upcoming legislative sessions in early 2027 will see lawmakers in multiple states introduce bills establishing strict ratepayer protection rules, mandatory grid impact studies, and local consent referendums. Cloud providers unable to meet local environmental and energy conditions may increasingly look to offshore jurisdictions or remote industrial zones with surplus captive energy supplies.
