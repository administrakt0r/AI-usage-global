## Bell Canada 1.2GW AI Campus Masks Heavy Indirect Water Demand

### University of Saskatchewan study warns closed-loop cooling ignores offsite coal and gas plant water consumption across the Assiniboine River basin

Bell Canada's planned 1.2-gigawatt AI Fabric campus outside Regina, Saskatchewan, promotes an on-site closed-loop cooling design that obscures the project's true environmental footprint, according to new research from the University of Saskatchewan. While the data centre avoids direct municipal and groundwater withdrawals on-site, supplying its massive 1.2-gigawatt electricity demand will force substantial cooling water withdrawals at distant coal and natural gas power plants across the drought-stressed Assiniboine River basin. The findings expose a major regulatory loophole in hyperscale sustainability pledges that evaluate data centre water efficiency in isolation from grid-level resource consumption.

## Key details

Doctoral researcher Corwyn Shomachuk of the University of Saskatchewan's Global Institute for Water Security detailed the facility's hidden hydrological strain in [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/data-centres-may-consume-far-more-water-than-developers-suggest-296245), subsequently republished by [Water Canada](https://www.watercanada.net/data-centres-may-consume-far-more-water-than-developers-suggest/). Situated in the Rural Municipality of Sherwood just outside Regina, the project encompasses over 1,000 acres following an additional 900-acre land acquisition. Bell Canada has promoted the campus as the single largest private-sector capital investment in Saskatchewan history, with total capital expenditure projected to surpass $50 billion CAD.

To power the facility, the initial phase requires 300 megawatts (MW) from the grid of provincial utility [SaskPower](https://www.saskpower.com). The subsequent 900 MW expansion relies on a dedicated on-site bring-your-own-power framework powered primarily by natural gas. Bell Canada notes that the server halls will use a sealed closed-loop water-cooling circuit paired with an air-cooled heat-rejection system, avoiding direct connections to municipal water or local aquifers.

However, grid realities undermine the zero-water claim. According to SaskPower's 2025–26 annual report, approximately 25 percent of provincial power capacity relies on coal. To accommodate soaring loads, authorities initiated life extensions for three coal-fired plants in southern Saskatchewan: Poplar River, Shand, and Boundary Dam. These thermal stations rely on evaporative wet cooling systems that draw heavily from regional reservoirs, such as the Rafferty Reservoir. Supplying 300 MW to the campus shifts continuous water consumption hundreds of kilometers upstream. Furthermore, gas extraction in Alberta to feed the remaining 900 MW demands intensive water volumes.

### By the numbers

- **1.2 GW**: total planned power capacity for Bell Canada's AI Fabric campus outside Regina
- **300 MW**: baseload electricity allocation drawn directly from SaskPower's provincial grid
- **900 MW**: planned on-site bring-your-own-power capacity, primarily gas-fired generation
- **25%**: share of Saskatchewan's electric power generation capacity derived from coal
- **$50B+ CAD**: estimated total private capital investment across all project phases
- **1,000+ acres**: total campus land footprint in the Rural Municipality of Sherwood

### Why this matters

The findings expose how hyperscale developers exploit local site boundaries to claim water stewardship while transferring ecological strain onto regional power grids. While air radiators and closed-loop liquid systems prevent on-site evaporation, generating thermal power consumes immense volumes of water for cooling towers and boilers.

In the Assiniboine River basin and Prairie Pothole region, freshwater supplies face severe seasonal swings and recurrent droughts. Agriculture and municipal reservoirs depend on erratic spring snowmelt. Funneling grid capacity into AI clusters accelerates reservoir depletion, intensifying regional competition for finite water resources.

## Risks and open questions

The project faces regulatory pushback. Local residents in Sherwood have raised concerns over fast-tracked rezoning and the lack of a comprehensive provincial environmental assessment.

Neither Bell Canada nor SaskPower has published life-cycle water withdrawal projections for the coal fleet or proposed gas turbines. Furthermore, Saskatchewan's plan to replace coal with small modular reactors (SMRs) on the Rafferty Reservoir in the 2030s will sustain heavy cooling water demands across the basin.

## What happens next

SaskPower and Bell Canada will negotiate final grid interconnection terms for the initial 300 MW phase. Sherwood municipal authorities are scheduled to review detailed building permits through 2027.

Researchers at the University of Saskatchewan plan to expand hydrological flow modelling across the Assiniboine River basin to track cumulative evaporative withdrawals. Initial data hall operations are slated for 2027, with the full 1.2 GW buildout extending through 2030.
