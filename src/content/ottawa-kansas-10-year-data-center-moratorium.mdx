## Ottawa, Kansas Approves 10-Year Moratorium on Large Data Centers

### Voters enact a decade-long ban on facilities over 25MW after halting a 450MW project

Voters in Ottawa, Kansas, have overwhelmingly approved a 10-year moratorium on large-scale data center developments. The ballot measure establishes a decade-long prohibition on any data center facility with an electric power capacity exceeding 25MW.

The measure passed with 71.85 percent support during the November 2026 midterm elections in Franklin County. The vote cements local protections following an 18-month community campaign against a proposed 450MW campus and gas-fired power plant.

## Key details

The new municipal ordinance bars the construction and operation of data centers demanding more than 25MW of capacity across the city of Ottawa for the next ten years. The citizen initiative was organized by Ottawa United, a grassroots coalition formed by local residents across party lines to oppose utility-scale digital infrastructure.

Public concern escalated when developer Lightfield Energy LLC sought to acquire a 300-acre site at Proximity Park from the Ottawa City Commission. The developer planned to construct a 450MW data center campus supported by an on-site natural gas power plant. Although the Ottawa City Commission terminated its land sale agreement with Lightfield Energy in September after failing to reach a development pact over 18 months, residents pursued the ballot referendum to prevent future hyperscale development.

Two neighboring Kansas communities, Emporia and El Dorado, also enacted data center moratoriums during the same election cycle. Nationwide, more than 40 similar restrictive measures and moratoriums appeared on local ballots as municipalities responded to escalating energy and water demands from computing infrastructure.

### Why this matters

The Ottawa referendum underscores growing voter resistance to large computing campuses in rural and exurban communities. Hyperscale facilities often seek municipal acreage to secure grid access and on-site generation, but municipal residents increasingly fear strain on regional electrical infrastructure, local air quality from gas turbines, and diversion of regional resources.

By establishing an explicit 25MW threshold for a full decade, Ottawa voters created one of the longest statutory pauses on data center development in the United States. This prevents tech developers and utilities from quietly renegotiating large-scale parcel deals behind closed doors.

### Context

As artificial intelligence workloads and cloud computing clusters expand, developers have actively sought sites outside traditional data center hubs like Northern Virginia and Silicon Valley. Midwestern states like Kansas have attracted interest due to available land and central connectivity.

However, the rapid influx of multi-hundred-megawatt proposals has prompted backlash over utility rates, grid stability, and local environmental impacts. The simultaneous approval of restrictions in Ottawa, Emporia, and El Dorado mirrors a broader trend across North America, where voters are rejecting hyperscale campuses regardless of anticipated municipal tax revenues.

## Risks and open questions

A critical question is whether long-term municipal moratoriums will redirect development pressure toward unincorporated county land or neighboring jurisdictions with fewer zoning protections. While Ottawa has closed its borders to facilities above 25MW, regional utilities in the Midwest must still navigate capacity planning and grid stability as developers scout alternative rural sites.

Additionally, municipal bans that cap capacity at 25MW leave smaller edge deployments permissible, raising questions about whether distributed clusters could eventually bypass large-scale restrictions.

## What happens next

The 10-year moratorium takes effect immediately under municipal code, barring Ottawa officials from accepting or approving applications for facilities above 25MW through late 2036.

Regional developers and grid planners are expected to re-evaluate site selection strategies across Kansas and the broader Midwest, while municipal watchdog groups monitor neighboring Franklin County jurisdictions for potential spillover proposals.
