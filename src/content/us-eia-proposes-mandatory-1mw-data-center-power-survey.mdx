## US EIA Proposes Mandatory 1MW Data Center Power Survey

### Federal energy agency seeks annual facility-level electricity and cooling reports from 1,400 US operators

The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) has proposed the nation's first mandatory federal survey of data center electricity consumption, targeting every U.S. facility with an IT design capacity of at least 1 megawatt (MW). Published in the Federal Register on November 27, 2026, [Form EIA-872](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/27/2026-24077/agency-information-collection-proposed-new-survey) would cover an estimated 1,400 respondents each year starting in 2027. Until now, federal energy statistics have lacked uniform, site-level metering for AI clusters, hyperscale campuses, and colocation halls.

## Key details

Under the notice for the Data Center Energy Consumption Survey (DCECS), EIA asks the Office of Management and Budget for authority under 15 U.S.C. 772(b) to require annual filings from each entity holding a facility's master electric utility contract. The scope spans operational and planned standalone buildings, shared buildings, and multi-building campuses across enterprise, colocation, hyperscale, edge, super intelligence (SI) data centers, carrier hotels, network gateways, and cryptocurrency mines.

Draft [Form EIA-872 respondent instructions](https://www.eia.gov/survey/form/eia_872/proposed/instructions.pdf) detail 25 reporting items across company, campus, and facility tabs. Operators must submit contracted utility load (in MW), actual average monthly peak demand for both the whole facility and IT equipment alone, IT equipment electricity consumption (in megawatt-hours), and purchased grid electricity. Respondents must also list on-site generation nameplate capacity, including behind-the-meter power purchase agreements and facility-serving batteries, and identify cooling methods across 10 categories, from direct-to-chip liquid and immersion tanks to open-loop evaporative cooling towers.

EIA estimates each annual filing will take an average of 8.7 hours, totaling 12,200 industry burden hours and $1,158,268 in annual compliance costs at $94.94 per hour. Non-compliance or false statements carry civil penalties of up to $13,273 per day, per facility, alongside potential criminal sanctions for willful violations.

### By the numbers

- **1 MW**: Minimum IT design capacity (1,000 kilowatts of server, storage, and networking capacity) required to trigger mandatory facility or campus reporting.
- **1,400**: Estimated annual respondents and total responses expected across U.S. data center operators.
- **12,200 hours**: Projected annual reporting burden nationwide, averaging 8.7 hours per respondent and $1,158,268 in total compliance cost.
- **$13,273**: Maximum daily civil penalty per facility for failing to file or submitting false information.
- **2018**: Reference year of EIA's last Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey (CBECS), which the agency says cannot capture continuous IT loads.

### Why this matters

Grid operators and state regulators currently rely on fragmented utility forecasts and corporate sustainability reports to estimate AI power demand. Because Form EIA-872 separates IT electricity use from total purchased and on-site power, the agency will calculate Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) directly from metered facility data rather than self-reported averages. Collecting planned capacity alongside active contracted loads will also give transmission planners clearer visibility into upcoming grid additions across state and Census division lines.

### Context

EIA previously tested data center tracking in a 50-building [2018 CBECS Data Center Pilot](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/commercial/reports/2018/pilot/), concluding that existing commercial building samples lacked an adequate facility frame. In May 2026, an [EIA Long-Term Outlook analysis](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67704) estimated that data center servers consumed 7% of U.S. commercial sector electricity in 2025 and projected server demand could reach 22% to 33% of commercial electricity by 2050. Congressional proposals such as [the federal POWER Act](https://aiusageglobal.pages.dev/blog-detail/federal-bill-proposed-to-track-ai-data-center-water-and-power-use) have also sought mandatory DOE reporting on large facilities' power and water consumption.

## Risks and open questions

Form EIA-872 leaves major parts of AI's resource footprint unmeasured. Although the form asks whether facilities run open-loop evaporative cooling towers or closed-loop adiabatic coolers, it collects no water withdrawal or evaporative consumption volumes. It also does not require operators to separate AI training or inference loads from conventional cloud hosting or cryptocurrency mining within the same facility, nor does it mandate direct megawatt-hour metering of on-site gas or diesel generation beyond nameplate capacity and generation type. Under the Confidential Information Protection and Statistical Efficiency Act of 2018 (44 U.S.C. 3572), facility-level filings will remain strictly confidential, with public data released only in national, regional, and state aggregates.

## What happens next

Public comments on Form EIA-872 are open until January 26, 2027, giving utilities, trade groups, and environmental researchers 60 days to weigh in on the 1 MW threshold, facility definitions, and burden estimates. After reviewing feedback and securing Office of Management and Budget clearance, EIA plans to launch annual DCECS collection in 2027 covering calendar year 2026 operations.
