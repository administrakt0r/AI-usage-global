## IEA: AI data center energy use set to triple by 2030

### Electricity demand from AI-focused facilities grew 17% in 2025 as the sector scrambled to address physical infrastructure bottlenecks.

A new report from the International Energy Agency (IEA) reveals that electricity consumption from data centers is poised to double by 2030, with power use specifically from AI-focused facilities expected to triple in the same period. In 2025 alone, data center electricity demand soared by 17%, significantly outpacing the 3% growth in total global electricity demand.

## Key details

The report, "Key Questions on Energy and AI," highlights a massive surge in capital expenditure by five major technology companies, reaching more than $400 billion in 2025—a figure set to increase by another 75% in 2026. This investment is primarily directed toward AI-ready infrastructure as companies race to deploy agentic AI systems and large-scale models.

While the efficiency of individual AI tasks is improving at a rate unprecedented in energy history, the sheer volume of users and the rise of more energy-intensive AI applications are driving overall consumption higher. Data centers now account for approximately 40% of all corporate power purchase agreements (PPAs), as firms scramble to secure carbon-free energy sources amid tightening supply chains for transformers, gas turbines, and specialized chips.

### Why this matters

The tripling of AI energy demand by 2030 presents a significant challenge for global power grids and climate goals. The "scramble for solutions" mentioned by the IEA underscores the physical limits of current infrastructure, where regulatory delays and grid connection backlogs are becoming as much of a hurdle as the availability of the hardware itself.

### Context

This report follows the IEA's 2025 analysis, showing that the resource trajectory for AI has not only continued but accelerated. The move toward agentic AI is particularly significant, as these systems often require sustained compute cycles compared to one-off inference tasks, further straining energy resources and requiring more advanced cooling and power delivery systems.

## What happens next

The IEA expects physical bottlenecks—including chip shortages and grid connection delays—to persist through 2026. Governments and utilities are likely to face increasing pressure to streamline approval processes for new data center projects while simultaneously mandating stricter energy efficiency and water reuse standards to mitigate the local impact of these massive facilities.
