## AI Data Center Backup Power Drives $21 Billion Health Cost Surge

### Report reveals temporary turbines and backup generators release dangerous emissions across the United States.

A new report from the Environmental Protection Network reveals that air pollution driven by AI data center growth could increase U.S. healthcare costs by nearly $21 billion by 2028. The surge in emissions, fueled largely by federal regulatory easing around on-site backup generators and temporary gas turbines, is projected to cause roughly 600,000 asthma cases and 1,300 premature deaths nationwide.

## Key details

The report, titled *Hidden Health Costs of AI Data Centers*, evaluates 30 federal regulatory decisions that have permitted increased air pollution to accommodate rapidly rising AI power demand. While part of the health impact stems from extended coal and gas plant operations, a major driver is the widespread deployment of unmonitored on-site backup generation and mobile combustion turbines at hyperscale facilities.

Key findings from the report highlight the local and national health consequences of AI backup power:
- **National Health Burden:** Air pollution from data centers is estimated to create nearly $21 billion in additional public health expenditures by 2028, contributing to approximately 600,000 asthma symptom episodes and 1,300 premature deaths.
- **Virginia Local Impact:** In Virginia, home to the world's largest concentration of data centers, on-site backup generators operating at just 10% of their permitted capacity limits are projected to cause 14,000 asthma symptom cases, 13 to 19 premature deaths, and $220 million to $300 million in annual health-related costs.
- **Regulatory Easing and NOx Spikes:** A rule finalized by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency created a category for small and medium temporary turbines that can operate on-site for up to two years with reduced monitoring and reporting. This regulatory change is expected to increase annual nitrogen oxide (NOx) emissions by 89% compared to earlier proposed limits.
- **Demand-Response and Emergency Orders:** Federal rules allow data center operators to run dirty backup diesel engines and gas turbines during grid demand-response events and emergency grid alerts issued by regional transmission operators like PJM Interconnection.

### Why this matters

As electric utilities struggle to supply grid power fast enough for gigawatt-scale AI workloads, data center developers are increasingly turning to behind-the-meter generation, diesel generators, and temporary gas turbines. Because these engines emit high volumes of smog-forming volatile organic compounds, soot, and nitrogen oxides directly at the facility site, nearby residential communities face severe localized air quality degradation and heightened health risks without public notice or standard air permit checks.

### Context

The reliance on temporary backup generation has already sparked high-profile legal battles across the country, including lawsuits against xAI in Mississippi over unpermitted turbine usage. With federal emergency orders authorizing emergency generator runs during heat waves and peak demand periods, the distinction between temporary backup generation and continuous power production is rapidly blurring across the AI infrastructure sector.

## Risks and open questions

A central open question is whether federal or state courts will overturn recent regulatory exemptions granted to islanded and temporary power generation assets. If state environmental agencies or federal courts mandate stricter air permitting and continuous emissions monitoring for backup generator fleets, hyperscale developers could face operational restrictions or mandatory transitions to cleaner, higher-cost microgrids and battery storage systems.

## What happens next

Environmental groups and public health advocates are preparing legal challenges to federal regulatory rollbacks governing temporary turbine permits and emergency generator usage. Concurrently, municipal governments and state utility commissions are considering local air quality mandates that would require data centers to disclose backup fuel consumption, install continuous monitoring hardware, or restrict generator operation near residential zones.
