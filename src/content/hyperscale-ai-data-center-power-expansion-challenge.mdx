## Hyperscale AI Power Demand Reaches Critical Bottlenecks

### Industrial Info Resources data shows $7 trillion in global data center proposals as grid queues and turbine shortages stretch timelines to 2034.

An unprecedented surge in artificial intelligence infrastructure spending is driving massive utility queue backlogs and severe hardware equipment shortages across North America. According to new data from Industrial Info Resources presented at Data Center World Power, combined capital expenditures by hyperscalers Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Google have jumped tenfold from $64 billion in 2018 to over $700 billion in 2026.

## Key details

Global data center proposals announced since April 2026 have consistently surpassed $400 billion per month, pushing cumulative worldwide capital commitments past $7 trillion. In the United States and Canada, the active development pipeline encompasses more than 4,250 planned or active projects representing $4.75 trillion in investment and over 400 GW of proposed electricity demand. Texas alone accounts for nearly $1.25 trillion of this planned infrastructure. Nearly 100 individual sites across the United States are being designed for capacities of at least 1 GW.

Operational U.S. data center power demand has reached 53 GW, up from 23 GW in 2023, with another 42 GW currently approved or under construction. U.S. electricity grid planners have revised decade-ahead growth projections upward nearly sevenfold, from 24 GW anticipated in 2022 to 166 GW in 2025.

Local opposition to the rapid industrialization of land and resources has escalated sharply. More than 500 local data center moratoriums are now recorded across 42 U.S. states, led by New Jersey with 41 and Michigan with 36. In the first quarter of 2026 alone, $130 billion in data center projects were rejected or canceled—exceeding the total rejections for all of 2025.

Equipment supply chains face severe constraints. Major gas turbine manufacturers including GE Vernova, Mitsubishi Power, and Siemens Energy are completely sold out of large-frame turbines through 2029, with delivery lead times extending into 2031. With public utility grid interconnection queues stretching out to 2034 for demands exceeding 300 MW, developers are increasingly funding on-site natural gas power plants, fuel cells, reciprocating engines, and small modular nuclear reactors to secure dispatchable electricity.

### Why this matters

The sheer scale of AI compute scaling has turned power generation and transmission capacity into the primary physical constraint for technological expansion. As utility interconnection wait times stretch past a decade, hyperscalers and developers are forced to become independent power producers, fundamentally altering energy markets and accelerating reliance on behind-the-meter fossil fuels and alternative microgrid generation.

### Context

While renewable energy projects like solar paired with battery energy storage systems (BESS) continue to grow—with 701 BESS projects worth $200 billion currently in development—the immediate need for continuous 24/7 baseload power has re-energized gas infrastructure investment. Industrial Info Resources estimates that $228 billion in natural gas power projects and $311.4 billion across 111 nuclear projects (including 61 small modular reactors) are currently being pursued to keep pace with hyperscale AI scaling demands.

## Risks and open questions

The rapid push toward behind-the-meter fossil fuel generation presents significant long-term risks. Developers face volatile fuel prices and potential regulatory penalties, while local communities express growing alarm over localized air emissions, noise pollution, and water table depletion. Furthermore, with $130 billion in project rejections in Q1 2026 alone, escalating community opposition poses an increasing threat to project execution.

## What happens next

Expect hyperscalers to expand direct investments in self-funded generation infrastructure, including natural gas, fuel cells, and nuclear power agreements. Regulators and state legislatures are likely to face intensified pressure to enact ratepayer protection mandates and stricter environmental oversight as grid interconnections remain bottlenecked well into the 2030s.
